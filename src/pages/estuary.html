Summary

An estuary is a partly enclosed body of water where a river meets the sea. Fresh and salt water mix into brackish water, and the salinity changes from place to place and with every tide.{{cite:noaa_est01}} Estuaries are some of the most fertile ecosystems on Earth.{{cite:noaa_est03}}

1

Conditions: salinity, tides and seasons

Salinity, not climate alone, decides what can live in each part of an estuary.

  • Salinity: the ocean averages about 35 ppt (parts per thousand) of salt. Estuary water ranges from 0.5 to 35 ppt, and changes day to day with the tides and weather.{{cite:noaa_est01}}
  • Upper, middle and lower estuary: in the Chesapeake Bay the head of the bay and its tidal rivers are nearly fresh (under 0.5 ppt), most of the middle is brackish, and the mouth reaches 25–30 ppt.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
  • Tides: most places have two high and two low tides each day, which push salt water in and out.{{cite:noaa_est01}}
  • Layers: fresh river water is lighter, so it flows seaward on top, while denser salt water creeps upstream along the bottom. Where they meet, sediment and nutrients are trapped, creating a nursery area for young fish.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
  • Seasons: the water is fresher in spring, when snowmelt and rain swell the rivers, and saltier in dry months. Because the Chesapeake is shallow, its water ranges from about 34 °F in winter to 84 °F in summer.{{cite:cbp_physical}}

2

Bottom substrate

Soft mud and marsh peat: full of nutrients, short on oxygen.

  • Mud: mudflats are made of fine silt and clay that settle out in the calm water.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
  • Peat: under the marsh lies peat, a spongy, waterlogged layer of roots and partly decayed plants that can be several feet thick.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
  • Low oxygen: because the peat is waterlogged and full of decaying material, it has very little oxygen. Bacteria living there give marshes their rotten-egg smell.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
  • Nutrients: rivers carry nutrients from the whole watershed into the estuary, which is why marsh plants grow so thickly.{{cite:noaa_est03}}
  • Marsh zones: the low marsh floods at every high tide, while the high marsh, only a few centimeters higher, floods about twice a month during the highest tides.{{cite:noaa_est06}}

3

Where it is found

Wherever a river meets the ocean, at every latitude.

  1. Chesapeake Bay, United States
  2. San Francisco Bay, United States
  3. Amazon River mouth, Brazil
  4. Thames Estuary, United Kingdom
  5. St. Lawrence Estuary, Canada
  6. Columbia River, United States
  7. Río de la Plata, Argentina and Uruguay
  8. Gironde Estuary, France
  9. Elbe Estuary, Germany
  10. Congo River mouth, Central Africa
  11. Knysna Estuary, South Africa
  12. Sundarbans, Bangladesh and India
  13. Yangtze River mouth, China
  14. Murray Mouth, Australia
  • Why there: an estuary needs only a river flowing into the sea, so estuaries are found along coasts at every latitude. Where they form depends on rivers and the shape of the coastline, not on latitude.
  • Latitude changes the plants: salt marshes fringe estuaries in temperate and cold regions, while mangrove forests replace them in the tropics, where the water never gets colder than about 16 °C.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

Smooth cordgrass

Spartina alterniflora

Producer

The tall grass that covers the low marsh, flooded by salt water at every high tide. Its roots and dead leaves build the peat that the whole marsh stands on.{{cite:noaa_est06}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Salt filters and salt glands

Salt water floods the plant every day, and too much salt pulls water out of cells.

Special filters in the roots keep out much of the salt, and glands in the leaves push the extra salt out, where it dries into crystals.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

Cordgrass can live in salty mud where almost no other plants survive, so it faces little competition.

Air channels to the roots

The waterlogged mud has almost no oxygen for roots.

Spongy tissue called aerenchyma forms hollow air channels from the leaves down to the roots, and the plant makes more of it when flooded.{{cite:usda_spal}}

Oxygen reaches the roots so they can keep respiring while buried in airless mud.

Spreading underground stems

Tides and waves can wash plants and mud away.

Cordgrass spreads mainly by long, hollow underground stems (rhizomes) that send up new shoots.{{cite:usda_spal}}

The plant spreads quickly without seeds, and its dense roots hold the mud in place, which is why it is planted to stop shoreline erosion.{{cite:usda_spal}}

{{figure:estuary/eastern-oyster|species}}

Eastern oyster

Crassostrea virginica

Filter feeder and reef builder

Oysters grow on top of one another to form reefs that shelter many fish and invertebrates, and they filter plankton out of the water as they feed.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Closing up and switching how it breathes

Low tide or a rush of fresh water can leave an oyster in air or in water that is not salty enough, and it cannot move away.

The oyster closes its shell, stops feeding and switches from aerobic to anaerobic respiration (making energy without oxygen). It opens again when salty, oxygen-rich water returns.{{cite:noaa_est07}} Water trapped inside the shell lets it stay closed for a long time.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}

The oyster can wait out bad conditions in place, so it can live where salinity changes every day.

Filter feeding

Its food is microscopic plankton floating in the water.

The oyster pumps water over its gills, which trap plankton.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}

It feeds without moving, and its filtering also helps clean the water for other species.

Swimming larvae that settle on shells

Adults are cemented in place, and soft mud is a poor place to live.

A female releases about 100 million eggs a year. The larvae drift for 2–3 weeks, then cement themselves to a hard surface such as an old oyster shell.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}

Huge numbers of drifting young spread to new places, and settling on shells builds reefs up out of the mud.

{{figure:estuary/blue-crab|species}}

Blue crab

Callinectes sapidus

Predator and scavenger

Blue crabs eat almost anything, including clams, oysters, smaller crabs and dead fish, and are eaten by striped bass, herons and sea turtles.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Migrating to the right salinity

Blue crab larvae need water saltier than 30 ppt, found only near the ocean.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

After mating in brackish water, females travel to the salty mouth of the bay to release their eggs. The larvae grow in the ocean, then the young crabs ride nighttime high tides back into the estuary.{{cite:noaa_est07,cbp_bluecrab}}

Each stage of life ends up in the salinity it needs, using the tides as free transportation.

Handling a wide range of salinity

Water ranges from nearly fresh upstream to salty at the mouth.

The crab’s gills actively control the salt in its blood (osmoregulation). Adult males live upstream in low-salinity water, while females stay near the salty mouth.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

Blue crabs can use almost the whole estuary, though tolerating so many salinities costs energy.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

Paddle-shaped back legs

Food, mates and the right salinity are spread across open water.

The last pair of legs is flattened into paddles for swimming.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab}}

Blue crabs can swim long distances, chase prey and escape predators, not just walk on the bottom.

{{figure:estuary/fiddler-crab|species}}

Atlantic marsh fiddler crab

Uca pugnax

Detritivore (eats dead plant matter)

Fiddler crabs dig burrows all through the cordgrass marsh. The burrows let air into the mud, and when scientists removed crabs from part of a marsh, cordgrass growth there fell by 47%.{{cite:bertness1985}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Burrows plugged at high tide

Every high tide brings in predators; every low tide exposes the crab to birds and drying air.

The crab digs a burrow 30–90 cm deep near grass stems and seals it with a mud plug when the tide comes in, then comes out to feed at low tide.{{cite:adw_uca}}

The crab is safe during each high tide and still gets to feed when the mud is exposed.

Sorting food from mud

Its food is a thin film of detritus mixed into inedible mud.

It scoops mud into its mouth, uses water from its gills to float off the edible bits, and spits the cleaned mud out as little balls.{{cite:adw_uca}}

The crab can live on the most plentiful food in the marsh: dead plant matter.

One giant claw

Males must attract females in a crowded colony.

A male’s major claw can make up half his body weight. He waves it and drums the ground with his legs to court females.{{cite:adw_uca}}

The biggest, most visible displays win mates, so the trait helps males reproduce.

{{figure:estuary/striped-bass|species}}

Striped bass (rockfish)

Morone saxatilis

Top predator fish

A top predator that eats menhaden, anchovies, worms and crabs, and can live 10–30 years. The Chesapeake Bay is its largest nursery on the Atlantic coast: 70–90% of the Atlantic population spawns there.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Swimming upriver to spawn

Eggs and young need fresh or slightly salty water, while adults feed in the salty bay and ocean.

From April to early June adults swim up tidal rivers to spawn in fresh or brackish water. Eggs hatch in 2–3 days, and young fish spend their first years in the river shallows.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}}

Young fish grow up in sheltered, food-rich water, while adults use the richer feeding grounds downstream.

Gills that work in fresh and salt water

Moving from river to ocean means switching from losing salt to gaining it.

The gills can absorb salt in fresh water and pump it out in salt water.

One fish can use the whole estuary, from spawning rivers to the open sea.

{{figure:estuary/osprey|species}}

Osprey

Pandion haliaetus

Top predator (fish-eating bird)

A hawk that lives almost entirely on live fish. Ospreys were poisoned by the pesticide DDT, which built up through the food chain, and their numbers rebounded after it was banned.{{cite:noaa_est09,cornell_osprey}}

AdaptationChallengeHow it worksWhy it helps

Feet built for slippery fish

Wet, wriggling fish are hard to hold.

An outer toe that can turn backward, so the osprey grips with two toes in front and two behind, plus barbed pads on the soles of its feet.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}

Fewer fish slip away, so the osprey and its chicks get more food.

Feet-first dives

Its prey is underwater.

The osprey spots fish from the air, then dives with its feet stretched out in front to grab them.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}

Shallow, open estuary water full of fish makes this an efficient way to hunt.

High nests and long migrations

Eggs need safety from predators, and winters are cold and fishless.

Ospreys build nests on tall perches, including artificial platforms, and migrate long distances; one bird may fly more than 160,000 migration miles in its lifetime.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}

Nests stay out of reach, and migration keeps the bird near open water and fish all year.

5

Food web and ecology

A food web built on plankton, marsh grass and decaying plants.

  • Energy flow: phytoplankton and marsh grasses capture sunlight. Plankton feed oysters and small fish; dead cordgrass breaks down into detritus that fiddler crabs and other bottom animals eat.{{cite:adw_uca}} Crabs and small fish are eaten by striped bass and birds such as ospreys.
  • Productivity: rivers deliver nutrients from the entire watershed, making estuaries some of the most fertile ecosystems on Earth.{{cite:noaa_est03}}
  • Key relationships: fiddler crab burrows let air into the mud and help cordgrass grow, and oyster reefs provide shelter for many fish and invertebrates.{{cite:bertness1985,cbp_oyster}}
  • Why it matters to people: salt marshes act like giant filters that trap pollutants and extra nutrients, and like sponges that soak up floodwater and storm surge.{{cite:noaa_est03}}

6

Human problems and solutions

Each problem is paired with a solution that addresses it, with a real example.

Problem
Solution

Agricultural runoff, fertilizer pollution, and excess nitrogen and phosphorus

Rain and melting snow wash manure and unused fertilizer off fields, carrying extra nitrogen and phosphorus into streams that drain to the estuary.{{cite:epa_ag}}

Agricultural runoff reduction

Farmers use the right amount of fertilizer at the right time, plant cover crops, keep strips of plants along streams to soak up nutrients, and fence livestock out of streams.{{cite:epa_ag}}

Example: in 2010 the EPA set a “pollution diet” limiting the nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment that six states and Washington, D.C. may send into the Chesapeake Bay.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}

Eutrophication and hypoxia

The extra nutrients feed huge algae blooms. When the algae die and sink, bacteria break them down and use up the oxygen. Water below 2 mg of oxygen per liter is hypoxic: fish and crabs flee, and oysters, which cannot move, may die.{{cite:cbp_do}}

Estuarine monitoring and conservation programs

Regular measurements of oxygen and nutrients show where dead zones form and whether cleanup is working.

Example: the Chesapeake Bay pollution diet uses two-year milestones, and the EPA assessed the states’ 2025 progress.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}

Industrial and sewage pollution

Factories, streets, sewage plants and overflowing sewers release toxic metals, pesticides, bacteria and viruses. Some chemicals, like DDT and mercury, build up at each step of the food chain, up to ospreys and people.{{cite:noaa_est09}}

Better wastewater treatment

Upgraded treatment and storage tunnels catch sewage overflows before they reach the water.

Example: London’s Thames, declared biologically dead in the late 1950s, now has a 25-km sewage tunnel that cut overflows into the river by about 86% in its first year.{{cite:thameswater_2026}}

Plastic pollution

Plastic litter breaks into smaller and smaller pieces. Pieces under 5 mm, called microplastics, can be mistaken for food by fish and birds.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}}

Pollution prevention

Stopping pollution at its source: less single-use plastic, keeping litter out of storm drains and banning the most harmful chemicals.

Example: after DDT was banned, osprey populations rebounded.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}

Coastal development and dredging

Homes, ports and cities are built along the shore. Draining, filling, damming and dredging (digging out channels for ships) have destroyed 38% of the wetlands along U.S. coasts.{{cite:noaa_est09}}

Example: about 85% of San Francisco Bay’s wetlands have been lost to development.{{cite:usfws_sfbay2023}}

Wetland restoration and habitat protection

Protecting the marsh that remains, and breaching old levees to let tides back into land that was once marsh.

Example: in 2023 a 300-acre former salt pond in San Francisco Bay was reopened to the tides, part of a 50-year plan to restore 15,000 acres.{{cite:usfws_sfbay2023}}

Sea-level rise and saltwater intrusion

As the sea rises, salt water moves inland and slowly poisons coastal trees, leaving “ghost forests.” In the Chesapeake, sinking land makes the rise worse.{{cite:noaa_ghostforest}}

Living shorelines

Shores are protected with marsh plants, sand, rock and oyster shells instead of concrete seawalls. They absorb wave energy, store floodwater and give wildlife habitat.{{cite:noaa_livingshorelines}}

Example: NOAA reports that during major storms, living shorelines hold up better than hardened shorelines.{{cite:noaa_livingshorelines}}

Overfishing

Taking oysters, crabs and fish faster than they can reproduce. For oysters, harvesting also removes the shells their young need to settle on.

Example: oysters in Maryland’s part of the Chesapeake Bay fell 99.7% since the early 1800s, from overfishing, disease and habitat loss.{{cite:wilberg2011}}

Sustainable fishing and oyster restoration

Catch limits and sanctuaries protect breeding animals, and new reefs give oyster larvae a place to settle.

Example: by 2020, about 358 acres of oyster reef had been restored in Maryland’s Little Choptank River.{{cite:noaa_oyster2020}}
