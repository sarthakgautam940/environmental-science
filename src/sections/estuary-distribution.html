Global map of locations and latitudes

Estuaries form wherever rivers reach the sea, so they occur on every inhabited continent and at almost every latitude. What changes with latitude is the vegetation that lines them.

Latitudinal range and why estuaries occur where they do

Rivers meeting the sea. Any coast where a river enters the ocean can host an estuary, from the equatorial Amazon to the subarctic. The map spans roughly 35° S to 54° N, and estuaries extend beyond both.

Rising seas built today’s estuaries. Most estuaries are geologically young, less than 10,000 years old. Coastal plain estuaries like Chesapeake Bay formed at the end of the last ice age, 10,000–18,000 years ago, when melting glaciers raised sea level and flooded low river valleys. Others form behind barrier islands (bar-built), in basins created by plate movements (tectonic, like San Francisco Bay) or in valleys carved by glaciers (fjords).{{cite:noaa_est04}}

Latitude decides the vegetation. Mangrove forests grow along tropical and subtropical coasts where sea-surface temperatures never drop below 16 °C, lining about two-thirds of tropical coastlines.{{cite:noaa_est07}} A satellite survey found mangroves in 118 tropical and subtropical countries and territories, with the largest share between 5° N and 5° S.{{cite:giri2011}} Toward the poles, salt marshes take over: a global saltmarsh dataset records them in 99 countries.{{cite:mcowen2017}} The bold 30° lines on the map mark the approximate boundary between these two kinds of estuary edge.
