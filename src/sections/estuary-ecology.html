Food webs, energy and nutrients

Rivers deliver nutrients, tides stir them, and a huge supply of plant growth feeds everything from plankton to ospreys, much of it after it dies and decays.

Food web and energy flow

Energy enters the estuary through two main groups of producers: microscopic phytoplankton drifting in the water, and rooted plants such as smooth cordgrass and eelgrass. Phytoplankton are the foundation of the aquatic food web, feeding zooplankton and filter feeders.{{cite:nasa_phyto}} Cordgrass is the main ingredient of marsh peat, so much of what it produces is not eaten alive but stored or broken down into detritus: bits of dead plant matter coated with bacteria and fungi.{{cite:noaa_est06}} Detritus feeds fiddler crabs, periwinkles, mussels and blue crabs, which makes the estuary food web strongly detritus-based.{{cite:adw_uca,cbp_periwinkle,cbp_bluecrab}}

Nutrient cycling: delivered by rivers, trapped by mud

Nutrients from the whole watershed flow into estuaries, where they support lush plant growth.{{cite:noaa_est03}} The estuary works like a trap. Where fresh and salt water meet, nutrients and sediment are mixed, resuspended and held in the zone of maximum turbidity, which young fish use as a nursery.{{cite:cbp_physical}} Salt marshes filter herbicides, pesticides, heavy metals and excess sediment and nutrients out of the water.{{cite:noaa_est03}} Animals move nutrients too: ribbed mussels filter the water and deposit their wastes on the marsh, adding nitrogen to the soil and boosting cordgrass growth.{{cite:bertness1984}} In autumn, the surface water cools faster than the bottom, and the two layers of the Chesapeake Bay can mix rapidly (often overnight), bringing nutrients back up from the bottom.{{cite:cbp_physical}}

The big idea

Nutrients are the estuary’s greatest gift and its greatest threat. In the right amounts they make estuaries some of the most fertile ecosystems on Earth; in excess they fuel algae blooms and dead zones (see 5.4).{{cite:noaa_est03,cbp_do}}

Key species and relationships

  • Foundation species: smooth cordgrass dominates the low marsh and builds the peat that the whole marsh stands on.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
  • Ecosystem engineers: eastern oysters build reefs that provide habitat for many fish and invertebrates, and filter plankton as they feed.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
  • Mutualism: ribbed mussels fertilize cordgrass while the grass anchors them, and fiddler crab burrows aerate the soil while cordgrass roots hold the burrows open.{{cite:bertness1984,bertness1985}}
  • Trophic cascade: blue crabs and terrapins eat marsh periwinkles. Without these predators, periwinkles can turn a productive marsh into a bare mudflat within eight months.{{cite:silliman2002}}
  • Farming: marsh periwinkles cultivate fungi on wounds they make in cordgrass, the first fungus farming found in the ocean.{{cite:silliman2003}}
  • Nursery habitat: eelgrass beds shelter juvenile and molting blue crabs, and 70 to 90% of the Atlantic striped bass population uses the Chesapeake Bay to spawn.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass,cbp_stripedbass}}

Biodiversity and productivity

Estuaries are among the most fertile ecosystems on Earth.{{cite:noaa_est03}} Yet relatively few species live there, because tolerating wide changes in salinity takes a lot of energy, and far fewer organisms are euryhaline (able to tolerate a wide salinity range) than stenohaline.{{cite:noaa_est07}} The result is an ecosystem with modest numbers of species but enormous numbers of individuals: dense oyster reefs, schools of hundreds of mummichogs and great colonies of nesting wading birds.{{cite:cbp_oyster,cbp_mummichog,cbp_gbheron}}

Ecosystem services: why it matters to people

NOAA describes salt marshes as enormous filters and as buffer zones that stabilize shorelines and protect communities from floods and storm surge by soaking up water like a sponge.{{cite:noaa_est03}} A global survey of salt marshes in 99 countries highlights their value for fish production, carbon storage and coastal protection.{{cite:mcowen2017}} Estuaries also support fisheries for oysters, blue crabs and striped bass.{{cite:cbp_oyster,cbp_bluecrab,cbp_stripedbass}}

  • Water filtering: marshes and oysters remove sediment, nutrients and pollutants
  • Storm protection: marshes absorb floodwater and storm surge
  • Food: oysters, blue crabs and fish raised in estuary nurseries
  • Carbon storage: carbon locked away in marsh peat
  • Wildlife habitat: nesting and feeding grounds for ospreys, herons and egrets
