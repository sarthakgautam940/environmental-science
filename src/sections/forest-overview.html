Intro

A forest shaped by winter

The largest forest belt on Earth runs almost unbroken across North America and Eurasia. Its trees, animals and soils are all answers to the same set of problems: long cold, deep snow, a short summer and scarce nutrients.

Fly over northern Canada, Scandinavia or Siberia and the land below is a dark-green mosaic of conifers, lakes and bogs that can stretch for hundreds of kilometers. This is the evergreen coniferous forest, known to ecologists as the boreal forest (after Boreas, the Greek north wind) and in Russia as the taiga. UC Berkeley's biome guide describes it as the largest terrestrial biome, spread across a broad belt of Eurasia and North America.{{cite:ucmp_forests}}

The boreal forest has a simple structure compared with a tropical rainforest. A few cold-hardy conifers (spruces, firs, pines and larches) dominate the canopy, and the dense evergreen canopy lets little light reach the ground, so the understory is limited.{{cite:ucmp_forests}} Beneath them lies a carpet of mosses, lichens and low shrubs over thin, acidic soil. Farther south and along mild, wet coastlines, the same evergreen conifers form temperate coniferous forests, such as the giant rainforests of the Pacific Northwest. This page covers both, but focuses on the boreal forest, the classic “evergreen coniferous forest” biome.

The big idea

Almost every adaptation on this page traces back to four environmental pressures: (1) months of temperatures far below freezing, (2) a deep, long-lasting snowpack, (3) a growing season only about four months long, and (4) cold, acidic, nutrient-poor soils. Fire is a fifth, recurring force that resets the forest.

Quick reference for the class summary sheet, details and sources below

Type
Terrestrial biome (boreal forest / taiga; temperate conifer forests at lower latitudes, in mountains and on wet coasts)
Location & latitude
Alaska, Canada, Scandinavia, Russia/Siberia; mostly ≈ 50°–70° N. No boreal forest in the Southern Hemisphere. Map ↓
Temperature
Long, very cold winters; short, mild summers. Fairbanks: −22 °C (−8 °F) in January to 17 °C (63 °F) in July; six months average below freezing. Climatogram ↓
Precipitation
Low: about 300–900 mm (12–35 in) a year across the biome; 296 mm at Fairbanks, mostly summer rain, plus about 164 cm (65 in) of snow.
Soil
Spodosols (podzols): acidic, leached, with a bleached gray E horizon over a reddish B horizon; peat (Histosols) in bogs; permafrost soils (Gelisols) in the north. Soil ↓
Dominant plants
Black spruce, white spruce, balsam fir, lodgepole pine, tamarack (larch); eastern hemlock in the temperate south-east
Key animals
Moose, snowshoe hare, Canada lynx, gray wolf, brown bear, boreal owl, red squirrel, woodland caribou
Key adaptations
Needle leaves with waxy cuticles and sunken stomata; conical crowns; freeze tolerance; serotinous cones; white winter coats; snowshoe feet; hibernation; food caching; asymmetric owl ears. Species ↓
Human problems
Logging, fragmentation (roads, seismic lines), climate change, larger and more frequent fires, insect outbreaks, oil sands mining, caribou decline
Solutions
Certified sustainable forestry, replanting, protected areas, wildlife crossings, reclamation, prescribed fire, climate-matched seed transfer
