Global map of locations and latitudes

The boreal forest circles the Northern Hemisphere between roughly 50° and 70° N. Temperate conifer forests appear farther south, in mountains and along wet coasts.

How big is it?

Measured on an equal-area projection, the RESOLVE “Boreal Forests/Taiga” biome covers about 15.4 million km² and the “Temperate Conifer Forests” biome about 3.8 million km² (our calculation from the dataset; the boreal figure includes lakes, wetlands and open woodland within the forest zone).{{cite:resolve_data}} Canada alone holds 28% of the world’s boreal zone, 552 million hectares, including 270 million hectares of boreal forest.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}} Most of the rest lies in Russia, especially Siberia, with smaller areas in Alaska and Scandinavia.{{cite:ucmp_forests}}

Why the forest grows where it does

The northern edge is set by summer warmth. Trees need enough summer heat to grow and ripen seeds. Where the warmest month averages below about 10 °C, Köppen’s climate scheme switches from “cold” (D) to “polar” (E) climates, and tundra replaces forest.{{cite:peel2007}}

The southern edge is set by competition. Farther south, growing seasons lengthen to 140–200 days, and broadleaf temperate forest takes over in moist regions.{{cite:ucmp_forests}} Conifers persist there mainly where conditions are harsh in other ways, on cold mountaintops, sandy soils or fire-prone sites.

Why only the Northern Hemisphere? Look at the map: between 50° and 70° S there is almost no land, only the tip of South America and the Antarctic Peninsula. The Southern Ocean occupies the latitudes where a boreal forest would otherwise grow.

Mountains and coasts. Because temperature falls with elevation, subalpine conifer forests grow at high altitude in the Rockies, Alps and Himalaya. Along the Pacific coast of North America, mild, extremely wet ocean air produces temperate rainforest dominated by conifers (compare the Quillayute climatogram in section 1).
