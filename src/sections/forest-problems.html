5

Specific problems caused by humans

The boreal forest is remote, but it is not untouched. Logging, energy development and a rapidly warming climate are changing it, often in ways that interact.

A global satellite analysis of forest change in the early 21st century found that the boreal forest had the second-highest rate of forest loss on Earth after the tropics, driven largely by fire and forestry.{{cite:hansen2013}} Each problem below is described by its cause, how it affects the environment, which organisms and processes are hit, its ecological consequences and a real example.

5.1

Deforestation and commercial logging

Cause
Global demand for lumber, pulp, paper and other wood products, much of it supplied from boreal forests in Canada, Russia and Scandinavia.
Environmental mechanism
Clear-cutting removes the canopy over large areas and builds road networks into roadless forest. Harvested stands regrow, but as young, even-aged forest; true deforestation means permanently converting forest to another use, such as mines or roads.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}}
Organisms & processes affected
Species that need old forest (such as boreal owls that nest in cavities in old trees and woodland caribou that avoid disturbed areas) lose habitat; soil carbon and moss layers are disturbed.{{cite:cornell_borealowl,mb_caribou}}
Ecological consequences
Old, structurally complex forest is replaced by younger stands; roads open the landscape to further development.
Real-world example
About 0.2% of Canada’s boreal forest is harvested each year, and provincial laws require all harvested public land to be replanted or regrow naturally.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}} Satellite data show boreal forest loss from fire and forestry second only to the tropics.{{cite:hansen2013}}

5.2

Habitat fragmentation

Cause
Roads, pipelines, trails and especially seismic lines, narrow corridors cleared to survey for oil and gas.{{cite:dabros2018}}
Environmental mechanism
Seismic lines were historically up to 10 m wide; newer lines are narrower (1.75–3 m) but far more numerous. Vegetation recovers slowly on them, leaving a lasting web of cuts through the forest.{{cite:dabros2018}}
Organisms & processes affected
Linear features give predators easier access into caribou habitat, making caribou more vulnerable to predation.{{cite:mb_caribou}}
Ecological consequences
Fragmentation changes predator–prey relationships and edge habitat across huge areas, with significant consequences for threatened woodland caribou herds.{{cite:dabros2018}}
Real-world example
Seismic-line density has increased drastically across oil- and gas-rich regions of Canada and the United States.{{cite:dabros2018}}

5.3

Climate change

Cause
Greenhouse gases released by burning fossil fuels and clearing land trap more heat in the atmosphere.
Environmental mechanism
High latitudes are warming fastest: since 1979 the Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the planet as a whole.{{cite:rantanen2022}} Warmer conditions shorten the snow season and lengthen summer drought.
Organisms & processes affected
Snowshoe hares whose color change is timed by day length face more days white on brown ground; snowpack duration is projected to fall by 29–35 days by mid-century in their range.{{cite:mills2013}} Moose begin to show heat stress at about −5 °C in winter and 14 °C in summer.{{cite:renecker1986}}
Ecological consequences
Species built for cold become mismatched with their environment, while fire and insect outbreaks become more likely (see 5.4 and 5.5).
Real-world example
In 2023, May–October temperatures across Canada averaged 2.2 °C warmer than the 1991–2020 normal, helping drive a record fire season.{{cite:jain2024}}

5.4

Changing wildfire patterns

Cause
Fire is natural in the boreal forest, but a warmer, drier climate, earlier snowmelt and drought are making fires more frequent and extensive; people also start fires.
Environmental mechanism
Earlier snowmelt and multi-year drought dry out fuels, so fires start sooner and spread farther.{{cite:jain2024}} Black spruce forests are especially flammable.{{cite:kelly2013}}
Organisms & processes affected
Fire usually kills black spruce, and very severe fires can destroy the seed stored in lodgepole pine cones.{{cite:feis_picmar,feis_pinconl}} Burning releases carbon stored in trees and organic soil.
Ecological consequences
If fires return before conifers can mature and produce seed, forests can shift toward deciduous trees; in the past, severe fires favored less flammable deciduous vegetation.{{cite:kelly2013}}
Real-world example
Charcoal in lake sediments from Alaska’s Yukon Flats shows that burning in recent decades exceeds the limits of the past 10,000 years.{{cite:kelly2013}} In 2023, about 15 million hectares burned in Canada (a national record) and more than 200 communities were evacuated.{{cite:jain2024}}

5.5

Insect outbreaks

Cause
Native insects such as the mountain pine beetle and spruce budworm periodically erupt; invasive insects such as the hemlock woolly adelgid arrive through human trade.{{cite:feis_abibal,fs_hwa}}
Environmental mechanism
Beetles and adelgids feed on living tissue and sap; when populations explode across large areas of similar-aged, susceptible trees, whole stands die.
Organisms & processes affected
Lodgepole pine (mountain pine beetle), balsam fir (spruce budworm, its most serious damaging agent) and eastern hemlock (hemlock woolly adelgid).{{cite:feis_pinconl,feis_abibal,fs_hwa}}
Ecological consequences
Mass tree death reduces timber supply and can speed the replacement of pine by other tree species.{{cite:bc_mpb2012,feis_pinconl}}
Real-world example
British Columbia’s mountain pine beetle epidemic had killed 53% of the province’s merchantable pine by 2012; in the worst year, 2004, beetles killed 140 million cubic meters of timber.{{cite:bc_mpb2012}} In the eastern United States, the spread of hemlock woolly adelgid has been linked to regional declines in hemlock.{{cite:fs_hwa}}

5.6

Mining and resource extraction

Cause
Demand for oil, minerals and energy. Alberta’s oil sands (part of the third-largest proven crude oil reserves in the world) lie within Canada’s boreal forest.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}}
Environmental mechanism
Surface mining removes all vegetation and topsoil to reach the oil sands; drilling (“in-situ”) projects disturb less land but still require wells, roads and seismic lines.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands,dabros2018}}
Organisms & processes affected
Forest, peatland and soil communities are completely removed in mined areas; fragmentation affects wide-ranging species such as caribou.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands,mb_caribou}}
Ecological consequences
Restoring a self-sustaining boreal ecosystem on mined land is slow; reclamation of tailings ponds and most disturbed land “is just beginning, and will take many years.”{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}}
Real-world example
Since 1967, oil sands mining has affected 895 km² of land, of which only 1.04 km² had been certified as reclaimed by regulators at the time of the federal report.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}}

5.7

Biodiversity loss

Cause
The combined effect of habitat loss, fragmentation, climate change and changing disturbance patterns.
Environmental mechanism
When disturbance spreads across a landscape, species that need large areas of mature, undisturbed forest lose ground, while edge-adapted species and their predators move in.{{cite:mb_caribou}}
Organisms & processes affected
Woodland caribou are considered an indicator of boreal forest health.{{cite:mb_caribou}} Cavity nesters like the boreal owl depend on old trees, and color-changing species like the snowshoe hare face camouflage mismatch.{{cite:cornell_borealowl,mills2013}}
Ecological consequences
Losing caribou and other sensitive species simplifies the food web and signals wider ecosystem damage.
Real-world example
Boreal woodland caribou are listed as threatened under Canada’s federal Species at Risk Act.{{cite:mb_caribou}}
