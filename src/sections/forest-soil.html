Characteristic soil types

Conifer needles, cold temperatures and draining water produce Spodosols (podzols) whose striking bleached layer tells the story of nutrients being washed downward.

Characteristic soil types

The signature soil of the evergreen coniferous forest is the Spodosol (called a podzol in Canada and Europe). The name comes from the Greek spodos, “wood ash,” after the ash-gray layer near the surface. The University of Idaho’s soil-orders guide describes Spodosols as acid soils with a subsurface accumulation of humus bound to aluminum and iron; they typically form in coarse-textured (sandy) parent material, develop a light-colored E horizon over a reddish-brown spodic horizon, and often occur under coniferous forest in cool, moist climates.{{cite:uidaho_spodosols}} In Canada, podzolic soils dominate sandy deposits on the Canadian Shield where yearly precipitation exceeds about 700 mm; in drier parts of the boreal zone, less-developed (Brunisolic) soils form instead.{{cite:soilsofcanada}}

Two other soils fill in the landscape. In flat, waterlogged lowlands, partly decayed plants pile up as peat; soils that are at least 20–30% organic matter and more than 40 cm thick are classified as Histosols.{{cite:uidaho_histosols}} In the far north, Gelisols contain permafrost within two meters of the surface.{{cite:uidaho_gelisols}}

The horizons, top to bottom

  • O horizon: a mat of needles, twigs, moss and partly decomposed organic matter. Podzols are often capped by thick organic (LFH) layers.{{cite:soilsofcanada}}
  • A horizon: a thin, dark mineral layer mixed with humus (often very thin or absent in podzols).
  • E horizon: the “eluviated” zone: a pale, ash-gray layer stripped of iron, aluminum and organic coatings by downward-moving water.{{cite:soilsofcanada}}
  • B horizon: the “illuviated” zone where the stripped materials are deposited: reddish-brown where iron dominates (Bf / Bs) or dark where organic matter dominates (Bh). These deposits can cement into hard layers.{{cite:soilsofcanada}}
  • C horizon: little-altered parent material, often sandy glacial sediment.{{cite:soilsofcanada}}

What podzolization is, and why it happens

Podzolization is the soil-forming process that creates these horizons.{{cite:uidaho_spodosols}} The Soils of Canada project describes it step by step:{{cite:soilsofcanada}}

  1. The sandy glacial sediments of the Canadian Shield, derived from igneous rock, are already acidic.
  2. Organic decomposition products from coniferous needle litter make the upper soil even more acidic.
  3. This intense chemical weathering releases aluminum, iron and other metal ions from minerals in the upper soil.
  4. The metal ions bind to dissolved organic compounds, forming complexes (chelates).
  5. Water draining down through the soil carries the complexes into the B horizon, where they are deposited, leaving behind the bleached gray E horizon.

The process needs three ingredients: acidic organic litter, a parent material that drains freely, and more water moving down through the soil than is pulled back up by evaporation and plants. The cool, moist boreal climate supplies the last condition.

Why coniferous forest soils are acidic

Part of the acidity is inherited from granite-like parent rock, and part is added every year by the forest itself: the decomposition products of conifer litter are acidic.{{cite:soilsofcanada}} Because the soil is naturally infertile and acidic, Spodosols must be limed before they can grow most crops.{{cite:uidaho_spodosols}}

Needles, decomposition and organic matter

Cold temperatures slow decomposition and nutrient cycling, so needles and moss accumulate faster than microbes can break them down, building a thick organic mat.{{cite:saupe_conifers,soilsofcanada}} This slow decay locks up nutrients but also stores enormous amounts of carbon. A study of boreal forest islands in Sweden found that 50–70% of the carbon stored in the soil came not from fallen litter but from roots and the fungi associated with them.{{cite:clemmensen2013}}

Nutrient availability and leaching

Leaching (the downward washing of dissolved substances) removes nutrients from the root zone and carries them toward the subsoil or groundwater. Combined with slow decomposition, this leaves boreal soils poor in available nutrients.{{cite:uidaho_spodosols,saupe_conifers}}

How the soil shapes the vegetation

Soil explains much of what grows where. On poorly drained, nutrient-poor, cold soils (especially over permafrost) black spruce dominates; its stands are the least productive boreal forest type, and most of its roots grow in the upper 20 cm of organic soil.{{cite:feis_picmar}} Cemented layers that form in some podzols can block roots and water, causing the soil above them to stay saturated.{{cite:soilsofcanada}} On better-drained soils, white spruce grows best.{{cite:feis_picgla}} And because nutrients are scarce, keeping needles for several years instead of rebuilding a full set of leaves each spring helps conifers conserve them.{{cite:saupe_conifers}}
