Characteristic climate

A subarctic, continental climate with a huge yearly temperature swing, little precipitation, a long snow season and a growing season of only about four months.

The overall pattern

Boreal winters are long, cold and relatively dry; summers are short, moist and moderately warm.{{cite:ucmp_forests}} Climatologists place most of the biome in the Köppen “D” (cold, or continental) group. By the Köppen–Geiger rules, a D climate has a warmest month above 10 °C but a coldest month at or below 0 °C; the subtype c (“cold summer”) has only one to three months averaging above 10 °C, and the subtype d (“very cold winter”) has a coldest month below −38 °C.{{cite:peel2007}} Where even the warmest month stays below 10 °C, the climate becomes polar (E) and the forest gives way to tundra.{{cite:peel2007}} That 10 °C summer threshold is a useful rule of thumb for the forest’s northern edge.

How to read it

The wettest months are July and August, the warmest part of the year, while winter months each deliver only 10–20 mm of water, almost all of it as snow. The shaded green column marks the period between the median last spring freeze (May 13) and first fall freeze (September 13).

Temperature

The defining feature is the enormous difference between seasons. Fairbanks averages −22.4 °C (−8.3 °F) in January and 17.2 °C (62.9 °F) in July, a swing of almost 40 °C. On an average January day the temperature climbs only to −17.4 °C and falls to −27.3 °C; on an average July day it ranges from 11.7 °C to 22.6 °C. The annual mean is −2.1 °C (28.3 °F), below freezing.{{cite:noaa_fai}} Laboratory studies show that cold-hardened boreal trees survive temperatures below −40 °C for long periods, and their tissues can even survive immersion in liquid nitrogen, a hint of how extreme boreal winters can be.{{cite:strimbeck2015}}

Precipitation: rain, snow and season

Across the biome, coniferous forests receive roughly 300–900 mm (12–35 in) of precipitation a year, about as much as many grasslands.{{cite:nasa_biomes}} Interior sites lie at the dry end: Fairbanks averages just 296 mm (11.67 in) of rain and melted snow a year. More than half of it falls from June through September, peaking at 57 mm in July. Snow can fall in every month from September to May and totals about 164 cm (64.6 in) a year; November is the snowiest month, at 32 cm.{{cite:noaa_fai,noaa_fai_ann}}

Because summer is cool and the ground is frozen or snow-covered for much of the year, this modest precipitation still keeps the forest floor moist; in low-lying areas, water collects into bogs and fens rather than draining away (see Soil).

Length of the growing season

Plants can only grow actively between the last killing frost of spring and the first of fall. At Fairbanks the median freeze-free season is 121 days, from about May 13 to September 13.{{cite:noaa_fai_ann}} UC Berkeley’s biome guide gives a typical boreal growing season of about 130 days, compared with 140–200 days in temperate forests.{{cite:ucmp_forests}} With so little time, a plant that had to grow a new set of leaves every spring would lose weeks of its short summer, one reason most boreal trees are evergreen (see Requirement 4).

Geographic influences on the climate

Latitude. At 50°–70° N, the sun stays low in the sky even in summer and barely rises in midwinter. Weak winter sunlight and long nights let the land radiate heat away, producing the bitter cold.

Distance from the ocean (continentality). Water heats and cools slowly, so oceans moderate nearby climates. Interior regions such as central Alaska, central Canada and Siberia are far from that moderating influence, so their winters are colder and their summers warmer than coastal places at the same latitude. Compare Fairbanks (interior) with Quillayute on Washington’s Pacific coast (chart below): Quillayute’s coldest month averages 5.0 °C and its warmest 15.6 °C (a range of only about 10 °C) while it receives 2,574 mm (101 in) of precipitation a year and has a 190-day freeze-free season.{{cite:noaa_qui}}

Elevation. Temperature drops with altitude, so conifer forests also grow far south of the boreal zone on high mountains, the Rocky Mountains, Sierra Nevada, Alps and Himalaya (see map).{{cite:dinerstein2017}}

Proximity to the ocean. Where moist ocean air meets coastal mountains, mild winters and heavy rain support temperate coniferous rainforest instead of boreal forest, the setting of the Pacific Northwest’s giant spruces, hemlocks and cedars.

Snow and frozen ground

Snow is both a hazard and a shelter. Its weight bends and breaks branches, and its depth makes travel exhausting for animals, pressures that shaped conifer crowns and the feet of hares and lynx. But snow is also an insulating blanket; many small boreal animals overwinter beneath it.{{cite:strimbeck2015}} In the coldest parts of the biome the ground never fully thaws: soils with permafrost within two meters of the surface (Gelisols) occur in high-latitude regions, and black spruce is most common on poorly drained sites underlain by permafrost.{{cite:uidaho_gelisols,feis_picmar}}
