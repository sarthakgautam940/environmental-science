6

Potential solutions

The most effective approaches copy natural processes (fire, regrowth, animal movement) rather than fighting them. Each solution lists the problems from section 5 that it addresses.

6.1

Sustainable forestry and certification

Addresses 5.1 5.7

How it works
Harvest rates and methods are planned so the forest regrows and keeps its ecological functions. Many boreal harvesting practices now imitate natural disturbances such as fire and insect outbreaks, which help the forest renew itself.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}} Independent, third-party certification checks that a forest company operates legally, sustainably and to world-recognized standards.{{cite:nrcan_cert}}
Problem it addresses
Reduces the long-term damage of logging and protects habitat features that wildlife depend on.
Real-world example
As of 2021, 158 million hectares of Canada’s forests were certified by independent programs.{{cite:nrcan_cert}}
Limits & trade-offs
Even well-managed harvests replace old forest with young forest, which does not suit every species; certification standards vary between programs.

6.2

Reforestation and restoration

Addresses 5.1 5.2 5.6

How it works
Planting seedlings, or making sure harvested areas regenerate naturally, restores forest cover. Restoration can also target linear disturbances such as seismic lines, which otherwise recover slowly.{{cite:nrcan_boreal,dabros2018}}
Problem it addresses
Rebuilds forest after harvest and helps close fragmenting corridors.
Real-world example
In Canada, provincial laws require that all areas harvested on public land be replanted or allowed to regrow to set standards.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}}
Limits & trade-offs
Planted stands take decades to centuries to develop old-forest features such as large trees and woodpecker cavities.

6.3

Protected areas and critical habitat

Addresses 5.2 5.6 5.7

How it works
Parks, conservation areas and legally protected “critical habitat” keep large, connected areas free of industrial development, so species that need undisturbed forest can persist. Parks also serve as scientific baselines.{{cite:nrcan_boreal}}
Problem it addresses
Prevents habitat loss and fragmentation in the most important areas.
Real-world example
Wood Buffalo National Park protects almost 4.5 million hectares just north of the oil sands, and Alberta’s 2012–2022 Lower Athabasca Regional Plan committed to conserving more than 2 million hectares of habitat.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}} For boreal caribou, critical habitat rules aim to keep at least 65% of each caribou range undisturbed.{{cite:mb_caribou}}
Limits & trade-offs
Protected areas cannot stop climate change or fire, and boundaries do not always match where animals move.

6.4

Wildlife corridors and crossings

Addresses 5.2 5.7

How it works
Fencing keeps large animals off highways, and overpasses and underpasses let them cross safely, reconnecting habitat that roads have split. Animals need to cross to find mates, food and shelter, and sometimes to escape predators.{{cite:parks_banff}}
Problem it addresses
Reduces road deaths and reconnects fragmented populations.
Real-world example
Banff National Park has 44 wildlife crossing structures (six overpasses and 38 underpasses) and 82 km of fencing on the Trans-Canada Highway. Since they were built, wildlife–vehicle collisions have dropped by more than 80%. Wary species such as grizzly bears took up to five years to start using them.{{cite:parks_banff}}
Limits & trade-offs
Crossings are expensive, and species differ: grizzlies, wolves, elk, moose and deer prefer high, wide, short structures, while black bears and cougars prefer long, low underpasses.{{cite:parks_banff}}

6.5

Responsible resource extraction and reclamation

Addresses 5.6 5.2

How it works
Companies must plan reclamation before a project begins, post financial security bonds, and return land to a self-sustaining ecosystem with local vegetation and wildlife after extraction ends.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}} Lower-impact methods shrink the footprint: an in-situ (drilled) oil sands project disturbs only 10–15% of the land of a similar-sized mine, and newer seismic lines are much narrower than old ones.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands,dabros2018}}
Problem it addresses
Limits the area destroyed by mining and restores it afterward.
Real-world example
The Government of Alberta requires oil sands companies to reclaim 100% of land after the resource is extracted; Syncrude’s Gateway Hill was the first area certified as reclaimed.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}}
Limits & trade-offs
Reclamation is slow: only about 1 km² of 895 km² disturbed had been certified reclaimed at the time of the federal report.{{cite:nrcan_oilsands}} Narrow seismic lines are built in much greater density, so their total impact may still be large.{{cite:dabros2018}}

6.6

Wildfire management and prescribed fire

Addresses 5.4 5.5

How it works
Prescribed fire is the controlled application of fire by experts under specified weather conditions. Burn plans set the temperature, humidity, wind, fuel moisture and smoke conditions under which a burn may proceed. The right fire at the right place and time reduces hazardous fuels, limits the spread of pest insects and disease, and recycles nutrients to the soil.{{cite:usfs_rxfire}}
Problem it addresses
Reduces the risk of extreme, uncontrolled wildfires near communities and restores the natural role of fire.
Real-world example
The U.S. Forest Service manages prescribed fires (and even some wildfires) to benefit natural resources and reduce future risk, summarizing the principle as “more prescribed fires mean fewer extreme wildfires.”{{cite:usfs_rxfire}} The photo shows a prescribed burn in Canada’s Banff National Park.
Limits & trade-offs
Safe burning windows are short, smoke affects air quality, and prescribed fire cannot offset a climate that is making whole regions drier.

6.7

Climate adaptation (and cutting emissions)

Addresses 5.3 5.4

How it works
Forest managers plant seedlings whose genetics match the climate a site will have during the trees’ lifetime, not only today’s, an approach called assisted migration. Scientists match the climate of a seed source to the planting site and move seed to keep pace with climate change.{{cite:bc_seedtransfer}} Reducing greenhouse gas emissions addresses the root cause.
Problem it addresses
Helps planted forests stay healthy and productive as the climate warms.
Real-world example
British Columbia’s seed-transfer standards are designed to ensure that its planted forests are adapted to their climatic environment over time.{{cite:bc_seedtransfer}}
Limits & trade-offs
Climate projections are uncertain, and assisted migration helps planted trees, not wild animals such as hares or caribou.
