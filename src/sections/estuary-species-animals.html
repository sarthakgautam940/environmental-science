4B Animals: two ways to handle salt

Estuary animals deal with changing salinity in one of two basic ways. Osmoconformers, such as oysters and mussels, let the salt concentration of their body fluids rise and fall with the water around them; their cells protect themselves by adjusting an internal pool of amino acids and ions.{{cite:pierce1982}} Osmoregulators, such as blue crabs and fish, spend energy pumping salts across their gills to keep their blood concentration fairly steady.{{cite:towle1976,marshall2002}} Animals that cannot regulate often protect themselves by behavior instead: closing a shell, sealing a burrow or moving away.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

{{figure:estuary/eastern-oyster|species}}

Eastern oyster

Crassostrea virginica

Role
Filter feeder; reef-building ecosystem engineer
Size
Usually 3 to 5 inches; up to 8 inches
Shell
Flat top valve, cupped bottom valve with a purple muscle scar
Ecological role

Oysters filter plankton from the water by pumping it through their gills. They attach to one another to form dense reefs (also called bars, beds or rocks) that provide habitat for many fish and invertebrates. They are also one of the Chesapeake Bay’s most important commercial species.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}

Clamming shut and switching to anaerobic respiration Behavioral and physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Low tide can expose oysters to air, or bathe them in water that is suddenly much fresher.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
Adaptation
Oysters close their shells, stop feeding and switch from aerobic respiration to anaerobic respiration, which does not need oxygen.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
How it works
A strong muscle holds the two valves tightly shut, sealing the oyster away from the bad water. The space inside the shell is always filled with water, so the oyster can survive a long time closed. Hours later, when the tide brings back saltier, oxygen-rich water, it opens up and resumes feeding and breathing.{{cite:noaa_est07,cbp_oyster}}
Why it helps here
An oyster is cemented in place and cannot swim away from a pulse of fresh water. In cool months oysters can even survive out of the water for extended periods, though freezing air kills them.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
Survival & reproduction
Waiting out stressful tides lets oysters live where salinity swings daily, across much of the estuary.
Cells that adjust to the water around them Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
When salinity drops, water flows into cells by osmosis and could burst them; when salinity rises, water flows out.
Adaptation
Oysters are osmoconformers: their body fluids follow the salinity of the water, and their cells control their own volume.{{cite:pierce1982}}
How it works
The cells of euryhaline osmoconforming invertebrates keep a large internal pool of free amino acids and also use inorganic ions. When the water freshens, cells release some of these dissolved particles, so less water rushes in; when it turns saltier, they build the pool back up.{{cite:pierce1982}}
Why it helps here
Conforming is cheaper than pumping salt, which suits an animal that cannot move.
Survival & reproduction
Cell volume control lets oysters survive the gradual salinity changes of seasons and storms.
Swimming larvae that settle on shell Reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Adults cannot move, and soft mud is a poor place to live.
Adaptation
Mass spawning and a free-swimming larval stage. A female can release about 100 million eggs a year.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
How it works
Spawning begins in early summer as temperature and salinity rise. Larvae drift as plankton for two to three weeks, then the “pediveliger” larva settles on hard material and cements its left valve in place to become spat.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
Why it helps here
Tides and currents spread larvae across the estuary, and settling on existing shell builds reefs upward, out of the mud.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
Survival & reproduction
Huge numbers of offspring make up for heavy losses to predators and disease. Many oysters also change sex: most under one year old are male, while most older oysters are female.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
{{figure:estuary/blue-crab|species}}

Blue crab

Callinectes sapidus

Role
Mobile predator and scavenger
Size
Shell up to 9 inches across
Lifespan
Mature at 12 to 18 months; few live past 3 years
Ecological role

Blue crabs eat nearly anything they can find, including clams, oysters, mussels, smaller crustaceans, dead fish and detritus, and are themselves eaten by striped bass, red drum, herons and sea turtles.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab}} By eating marsh periwinkles, they help protect cordgrass from overgrazing.{{cite:silliman2002}}

Gills that pump salt Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
In the fresher upper estuary, salts constantly leak out of the crab’s body into the dilute water.
Adaptation
Blue crabs are osmoregulators that keep a high sodium level in their blood even in low-salinity water.{{cite:towle1976}}
How it works
An enzyme in the gills, sodium–potassium ATPase, uses energy to pump sodium back into the blood. In crabs kept at 5 ppt, its activity was almost twice as high as in crabs kept at 34 ppt, and it increased within two and a half hours of moving crabs into low-salinity water.{{cite:towle1976}}
Why it helps here
Regulating lets blue crabs roam the whole estuary, from nearly fresh tidal rivers to the salty mouth.
Survival & reproduction
Adult males can live in low-salinity water upstream, while females favor the saltier water near the mouth.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
A life cycle that follows the salinity gradient Behavioral and reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Blue crab larvae (zoeae) need water saltier than 30 ppt to develop well, which is found only in the ocean.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
Adaptation
A migration timed to salinity: crabs mate in brackish water from May through October, then females move to the saltier lower bay to release their eggs.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab}}
How it works
A female carries an orange egg mass (a “sponge”) of 750,000 to 2 million eggs and releases larvae near the mouth of the bay. Larvae develop in coastal water for four to eight weeks, then return as megalopae, swimming up and down in response to light and tides and riding nighttime flood tides back up the estuary to seagrass beds and marsh edges.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab,noaa_est07}}
Why it helps here
Each life stage ends up in the salinity it needs, using the estuary’s own tides as transportation.
Survival & reproduction
Young crabs grow up in sheltered nursery habitats where they can feed and hide from predators.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
Paddle-shaped swimming legs Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Food and the right salinity are spread across a large body of open water.
Adaptation
The last pair of legs is flattened into paddles, behind three pairs of walking legs.{{cite:cbp_bluecrab}}
How it works
The paddles scull through the water like oars, so a blue crab can swim as well as walk on the bottom.
Why it helps here
Swimming lets crabs make long seasonal migrations, chase prey and escape predators.
Survival & reproduction
Mobility is what lets females reach high-salinity spawning water.
{{figure:estuary/fiddler-crab|species}}

Atlantic marsh fiddler crab

Uca pugnax (also listed as Minuca pugnax)

Role
Deposit feeder and burrowing ecosystem engineer
Size
Shell about 15 by 23 mm (males)
Burrows
30 to 92 cm deep in mud and sand
Ecological role

Fiddler crabs feed on detritus in marsh mud and dig burrows throughout cordgrass marshes. Their burrows improve soil drainage and oxygen levels and speed the decay of buried plant material; when researchers reduced fiddler crab numbers for one season, cordgrass production fell by 47%.{{cite:adw_uca,bertness1985}}

Burrows sealed at high tide Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Twice a day the marsh floods, bringing predators such as blue crabs and fish; at low tide crabs are exposed to birds, heat and drying air.
Adaptation
Deep burrows, often near grass stems, which the crabs plug with mud during high tide.{{cite:adw_uca}}
How it works
As the tide rises, a crab retreats into its burrow and seals the entrance with a mud plug, staying inside until the water drains away.{{cite:adw_uca}}
Why it helps here
Cordgrass roots give burrow walls support in soft mud that would otherwise collapse.{{cite:bertness1985}}
Survival & reproduction
A safe burrow lets the crab survive each flood tide and return to feeding and courtship at low tide.
A built-in tide clock Physiological and behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Food is available only while the mud is exposed, and the timing of low tide shifts by about an hour each day.
Adaptation
An internal tidal rhythm: fiddler crabs kept in the laboratory continue to show tidal cycles of activity.{{cite:bennett1957}}
How it works
An internal clock keeps time with the tides, so crabs are primed to emerge as the water falls, rather than waiting to discover the change.{{cite:bennett1957,adw_uca}}
Why it helps here
Being ready at the right moment maximizes feeding time during each brief low tide.
Survival & reproduction
More feeding time means more energy for growth and for courtship displays.
Sorting food from mud, and a giant signaling claw Structural and behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Most food on a mudflat is a thin film of detritus mixed with inedible sediment; mates must be found in a crowded colony.
Adaptation
Mouthparts that separate detritus from mud using water pumped from the gills; in males, one enormous claw that can reach half of body weight.{{cite:adw_uca}}
How it works
The crab scoops mud into its mouth, floats out the lighter edible particles and spits the cleaned sediment back out as pellets. Males wave the big claw and drum the ground with their legs to attract females.{{cite:adw_uca}}
Why it helps here
Decaying cordgrass keeps adding fresh detritus to the marsh surface, so food is always underfoot.
Survival & reproduction
Efficient feeding and conspicuous signals together raise a male’s chances of mating.
{{figure:estuary/ribbed-mussel|species}}

Ribbed mussel

Geukensia demissa

Role
Filter feeder; partner of cordgrass
Habitat
Partly buried among cordgrass stems in the intertidal marsh
Tolerance
Temperature range of at least 56 °C
Ecological role

Ribbed mussels filter the water at high tide and deposit their wastes on the marsh surface. Experiments in a New England marsh showed that mussels raise soil nitrogen and make cordgrass grow taller and produce more biomass, above and below ground.{{cite:bertness1984}}

Byssal threads anchored to cordgrass Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Tidal currents and waves can wash loose animals off the marsh.
Adaptation
Strong protein threads (byssal threads) that attach the mussel to the bases of cordgrass stems.{{cite:bertness1984}}
How it works
A gland in the mussel’s foot produces tough fibers that harden in water and glue to plant stems and to other mussels.
Why it helps here
Cordgrass roots provide a firm anchor in soft mud, and the mussel’s wastes in turn fertilize the grass: a two-way benefit (mutualism).{{cite:bertness1984}}
Survival & reproduction
Anchored mussels stay in place to feed every high tide.
Air-gaping and toughness out of water Behavioral and physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Living high in the intertidal zone means hours out of water, exposed to heat, drying and big swings in salinity.
Adaptation
Air-gaping, plus wide tolerance of temperature (a range of at least 56 °C) and salinity, extensive tolerance of drying out, and very heat-stable enzymes.{{cite:lent1969}}
How it works
At low tide the mussel opens its shell slightly to breathe air (aerial respiration) instead of staying sealed. Enzymes that keep working at high temperatures let its cells function on a hot mudflat.{{cite:lent1969}}
Why it helps here
These traits let ribbed mussels live higher in the marsh than most other bivalves.{{cite:lent1969}}
Survival & reproduction
Living high in the marsh puts the mussel out of reach of many aquatic predators.
{{figure:estuary/marsh-periwinkle|species}}

Marsh periwinkle

Littoraria irrorata

Role
Grazer and fungus farmer
Size
About 1 inch
Predators
Blue crabs, mud crabs, diamondback terrapins
Ecological role

Periwinkles graze algae and detritus on cordgrass stems and the marsh surface.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle}} Their numbers are controlled by predators: when blue crabs and terrapins were removed in experiments, periwinkles turned a productive marsh into a bare mudflat within eight months.{{cite:silliman2002}}

Climbing the grass with the tide Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Every high tide brings in hungry blue crabs, mud crabs and terrapins.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle}}
Adaptation
Periwinkles avoid predators by climbing up marsh grass stalks.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle}}
How it works
As the water rises, snails crawl up cordgrass stems and stay above the waterline until the tide falls, then return to graze.
Why it helps here
Aquatic predators can only reach what is underwater.
Survival & reproduction
Escaping each high tide lets snails live long enough to reproduce; they release eggs into the water, which hatch into free-swimming larvae.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle}}
Farming fungus on cordgrass Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Living cordgrass is tough, salty food.
Adaptation
Fungus farming, documented along 2,000 km of the Atlantic coast: periwinkles graze wounds into live grass mainly to prepare a surface for fungi, which they then eat.{{cite:silliman2003}}
How it works
The snails chew holes in cordgrass leaves and spread their waste across the cuts, which encourages fungal growth; fungi are their preferred food.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle,silliman2003}}
Why it helps here
Fungi turn hard-to-digest grass into richer food. This was the first fungus farming found in the ocean and outside insects.{{cite:silliman2003}}
Survival & reproduction
A reliable, home-grown food supply supports dense snail populations.
Exam tip: This is a trophic cascade: blue crabs eat periwinkles, periwinkles eat cordgrass. Overharvesting blue crabs may contribute to salt marsh die-offs across the southeastern United States.{{cite:silliman2002}}
{{figure:estuary/mummichog|species}}

Mummichog

Fundulus heteroclitus

Role
Small omnivorous fish; prey for larger fish and wading birds
Size
5 to 6 inches
Name
A Native American word meaning “going in crowds”
Ecological role

This hardy killifish of marsh creeks eats algae, plants, insects, worms, small crustaceans and mollusks, and it is eaten by larger fish, wading birds and seabirds. It often forms schools of hundreds.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}

Gills that switch direction Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Marsh creeks swing between nearly fresh and fully salty water, and between warm, low-oxygen and cool conditions.
Adaptation
The mummichog withstands changes in temperature and salinity, low oxygen and even pollution.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
How it works
Euryhaline fish rely on mitochondria-rich “chloride cells” in their gills. In salt water these cells pump excess chloride out using sodium–potassium ATPase and chloride channels; in fresh water different transporters take salt in.{{cite:marshall2002}}
Why it helps here
Being able to reverse salt transport lets one fish live through every tide and season.
Survival & reproduction
Few predators or competitors can follow it into harsh, changeable creeks and pools.
Laying eggs at the highest tide Reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Eggs in open water are eaten by many predators.
Adaptation
Females lay sticky eggs in empty mollusk shells or on dead vegetation when the tide is highest, around the new or full moon.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
How it works
When the tide recedes the eggs are left exposed to air, and they do not hatch until they are covered again by the next month’s highest tide.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
Why it helps here
Eggs stranded high in the marsh are out of reach of aquatic egg-eaters, and the tide cycle itself times hatching.
Survival & reproduction
Females can spawn up to 460 eggs as many as eight times in one season.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
Changing shade to match the surroundings Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Herons, egrets and larger fish hunt by sight in shallow, clear creeks.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
Adaptation
Barred, olive and brown coloring whose shading can change with the surroundings.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
How it works
Pigment cells in the skin darken or lighten so the fish blends with mud or vegetation.
Why it helps here
Camouflage reduces detection in shallow water where there is nowhere deep to hide.
Survival & reproduction
Most mummichogs mature in their second year and live about three years.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}
{{figure:estuary/striped-bass|species}}

Striped bass (rockfish)

Morone saxatilis

Role
Top predatory fish
Size
Usually 2 to 3 feet, 10 to 30 pounds
Lifespan
10 to 30 years
Ecological role

Striped bass are among the top predators of the Chesapeake food web, eating menhaden, anchovies, worms, squid and crustaceans. The bay is the largest striped bass nursery on the Atlantic coast: 70 to 90% of the Atlantic population uses it to spawn.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}}

Swimming upstream to spawn Behavioral and reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Eggs and larvae need fresh or slightly brackish water, while adults feed in the bay and ocean.
Adaptation
A semi-anadromous life cycle: adults move from salty water into tidal rivers to spawn, from April to early June.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}}
How it works
Females lay eggs in fresh or brackish water near shore; the eggs hatch in two to three days and the larvae drift slowly downstream. Juveniles spend summer in the shallows of tidal rivers, and most stay in their home river for two years.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}}
Why it helps here
The estuary provides both a low-salinity nursery and rich adult feeding grounds within one connected system.
Survival & reproduction
Young fish grow in sheltered, food-rich rivers before joining the adults.
Gills that work in fresh and salt water Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Migrating between rivers, bay and ocean means facing opposite salt problems: losing salt in fresh water, gaining it in the sea.
Adaptation
Osmoregulation by the gills, which can either take up or secrete salt.{{cite:marshall2002}}
How it works
In seawater, chloride cells actively secrete chloride; in fresh water, the gills absorb sodium and chloride using other transport proteins.{{cite:marshall2002}}
Why it helps here
Switching gill function lets one fish use the full length of the estuary.
Survival & reproduction
Access to the whole salinity range links feeding grounds and spawning grounds.
Why it matters: Overfishing, habitat loss, pollution and low oxygen all threaten striped bass.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass}} In hot summers they face a “habitat squeeze” between surface water that is too warm and deep water without enough oxygen.{{cite:cbp_climate}}
{{figure:estuary/great-blue-heron|species}}

Great blue heron

Ardea herodias

Role
Wading predator of fish and other small animals
Size
Up to 4 ft tall; 6 to 7 ft wingspan; only 5 to 6 lb
Nesting
Colonies (rookeries) in swamps and on small islands
Ecological role

The great blue heron eats mostly fish but also insects, amphibians, crustaceans and other small animals, and spends about 90% of its waking hours hunting.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}

Stilt legs and a spear bill Structural and behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Prey hides in shallow, murky water that a heron cannot swim in.
Adaptation
Long legs, a long neck held in an S-shape, and a yellow, spear-like bill.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
How it works
The heron silently stalks prey in shallow water, then straightens its coiled neck to plunge its bill into the water and seize the prey.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
Why it helps here
Estuary shallows and mudflats, alternately flooded and drained by the tide, concentrate fish within reach of a wading bird.
Survival & reproduction
Steady hunting success supplies the food for chicks in the nest.
Hollow bones and colony nesting Structural and reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
A large bird must still fly, and its eggs and chicks are vulnerable to crows, ravens, raccoons, eagles and other predators.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
Adaptation
Hollow bones keep a 4-foot bird at only 5 to 6 pounds; herons nest together in rookeries in isolated wooded swamps and on small islands.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
How it works
Males build stick nests high in trees; both parents incubate 3 to 7 eggs for about 28 days, and young fledge after about 60 days.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
Why it helps here
Islands and swamps in and around the estuary limit access by people and predators.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
Survival & reproduction
Parents usually raise only one or two of their chicks, focusing food where it will succeed.{{cite:cbp_gbheron}}
{{figure:estuary/snowy-egret|species}}

Snowy egret and great egret

Egretta thula and Ardea alba

Role
Wading predators of fish and crustaceans
Snowy egret
27 in tall; black bill and legs, yellow feet
Great egret
39 in tall; yellow bill, black legs
Ecological role

Both egrets hunt fish, crustaceans and other small animals in estuary shallows and nest in colonies with herons and ibises on bay islands.{{cite:cbp_snowyegret,cbp_greategret}} They show how two related predators can share the same habitat by hunting in different ways.

Snowy egret: stirring up prey with yellow feet Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Small prey hides in soft mud and murky water.
Adaptation
An active hunting style: snowy egrets scurry through the water and use their feet to stir up the mud, then stab with their bills.{{cite:cbp_snowyegret}}
How it works
Stirring flushes hidden animals out of the sediment so the egret can see and catch them. Snowy egrets often forage in groups with other water birds.{{cite:cbp_snowyegret}}
Why it helps here
Mudflats and marsh pools are full of buried prey that a standing hunter would miss.
Survival & reproduction
A different technique from the herons reduces competition for the same food.
Great egret: patient stalking and stealing Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Competition for fish with many other wading birds.
Adaptation
Great egrets silently stalk prey in shallow water, then plunge their bills in; they also steal much of their food from smaller herons and egrets.{{cite:cbp_greategret}}
How it works
A long S-shaped neck and spear-like bill strike quickly from a standing position.{{cite:cbp_greategret}}
Why it helps here
Taking food from smaller birds turns competition into an opportunity.
Survival & reproduction
Females lay 3 to 5 eggs; both parents incubate them for about 24 days.{{cite:cbp_greategret}}
Conservation history: Great egrets were hunted nearly to extinction in the 19th century for their breeding plumes. The great egret became the symbol of the National Audubon Society, founded to protect birds from being killed for their feathers.{{cite:cbp_greategret}}
{{figure:estuary/osprey|species}}

Osprey

Pandion haliaetus

Role
Fish-eating bird of prey
Size
Up to 2 ft long; wingspan up to 6 ft
Chesapeake
Historically the world’s largest concentration of breeding ospreys
Ecological role

Ospreys feed almost exclusively on medium-sized fish such as perch, shad and menhaden.{{cite:cbp_osprey}} As top predators that eat fish, they were especially exposed to pesticides like DDT, which build up through the food chain (see 5.5); their populations rebounded after DDT was banned.{{cite:noaa_est09,cornell_osprey}}

Feet built to hold slippery fish Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Wet, slippery, struggling fish are hard to grip and carry.
Adaptation
A reversible outer toe, unusual among hawks, and barbed pads on the soles of the feet.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}
How it works
The outer toe can swing backward, so the osprey grips with two toes in front and two behind, while the barbed pads keep the fish from sliding out.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}
Why it helps here
Estuaries are full of schooling fish such as menhaden close to the surface.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
Survival & reproduction
A secure grip means fewer lost meals for the adult and its chicks.
Feet-first dives and a streamlined carry Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Fish are underwater, and a heavy catch makes flight harder.
Adaptation
Ospreys hover, then plunge into the water feet-first; back in the air they turn the fish to face forward.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
How it works
Hovering lets the bird line up on its target; a feet-first dive puts the talons in contact first. Carrying the fish head-first reduces drag.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
Why it helps here
Open, shallow estuary waters make fish visible from the air.
Survival & reproduction
Efficient hunting fuels the long migrations ospreys make: one bird may fly more than 160,000 migration miles in a 15- to 20-year life.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}
Faithful to the same high nest Reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Eggs must be kept safe from predators on a coast with few tall, isolated perches.
Adaptation
Ospreys usually pair for life and return to the same nest site year after year, building bulky nests on tall structures near water.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
How it works
Nests sit on dead snags, utility poles, channel markers and nesting platforms, out of reach of most predators.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
Why it helps here
Reusing a proven site saves time and energy each spring.
Survival & reproduction
Artificial platforms have become an important tool for bringing ospreys back to areas where they had disappeared.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}

Questions to be ready for

Likely questions about estuary adaptations, with short answers. Click a question to reveal the answer.

How does smooth cordgrass survive in salty, waterlogged mud?

Its roots filter out much of the salt and its leaves secrete the excess. Spongy aerenchyma tissue carries oxygen down to roots surrounded by oxygen-free mud.{{cite:noaa_est07,usda_spal}}

How do oysters tolerate changing salinity?

When the water turns too fresh they close their shells, stop feeding and switch to anaerobic respiration. Their cells also adjust internal amino acids and ions to control volume as salinity changes.{{cite:noaa_est07,pierce1982}}

What is the difference between an osmoconformer and an osmoregulator?

A conformer (oyster, mussel) lets its body fluids match the water. A regulator (blue crab, fish) uses energy, for example gill sodium pumps, to keep its blood concentration fairly steady.{{cite:pierce1982,towle1976}}

How do fiddler crabs cope with the tides?

They feed on exposed mud at low tide and retreat into burrows they plug with mud at high tide. An internal tidal clock times their activity.{{cite:adw_uca,bennett1957}}

Why do female blue crabs travel to the mouth of the bay?

Their larvae need water saltier than 30 ppt, found only near the ocean.{{cite:noaa_est07}}

How does the ribbed mussel help cordgrass?

It attaches to cordgrass with byssal threads and its wastes add nitrogen to the soil, increasing cordgrass growth.{{cite:bertness1984}}

Why do periwinkles climb cordgrass stems?

To escape predators such as blue crabs that arrive with the rising tide.{{cite:cbp_periwinkle}}

How is the mummichog’s egg-laying tied to the tides?

Eggs are laid at the highest tides of the month, sit exposed to air, and hatch when the next month’s highest tide covers them.{{cite:cbp_mummichog}}

What makes an osprey good at catching fish?

A reversible outer toe and barbed foot pads grip slippery fish; it dives feet-first and carries fish head-forward to reduce drag.{{cite:cornell_osprey,cbp_osprey}}
