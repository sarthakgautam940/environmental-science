5

Specific problems caused by humans

An estuary collects whatever its watershed sends downstream, and its shores are some of the most crowded land on Earth. Most of these problems are linked, so one action upstream can ripple through the whole system.

NOAA notes that estuaries are among the most fertile ecosystems on Earth, yet they may also be among the most polluted.{{cite:noaa_est03}} In the United States, 38% of the wetlands associated with coastal areas have been lost to draining, filling, damming and dredging.{{cite:noaa_est09}} Problems 5.1 to 5.4 form a single chain, from farm field to dead zone, so they are best read together.

5.1

Agricultural runoff

Cause
Rain and snowmelt wash manure, chemical fertilizer and loose soil off farm fields, and dissolved nutrients also leach down into groundwater that later feeds streams.{{cite:epa_ag}}
Environmental mechanism
Every stream in a watershed eventually drains to the estuary. The Chesapeake Bay’s watershed covers 64,000 square miles in six states and Washington, D.C., so runoff from farms hundreds of miles away ends up in the bay.{{cite:cbp_facts}} Soil particles cloud the water, and nutrients fertilize algae.
Organisms & processes affected
Underwater grasses such as eelgrass need clear water so light can reach the bottom; their health is used as a barometer of water quality.{{cite:dennison1993}} Phytoplankton respond to the extra nutrients with blooms.
Ecological consequences
EPA links nutrient runoff from farms to eutrophication, low-oxygen dead zones, fish kills, decreased aquatic life and harmful algal blooms.{{cite:epa_ag}}
Real-world example
The Chesapeake Bay’s pollution limits (see 6.1) target exactly the three things that run off fields: nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}

5.2

Fertilizer pollution

Cause
More fertilizer is applied to crops, lawns and golf courses than plants can use. EPA lists chemical fertilizer that crops do not fully use, and yard fertilizer from homes, as sources of nutrient pollution.{{cite:epa_sources}}
Environmental mechanism
Unused fertilizer dissolves in runoff and drains toward the estuary; NOAA specifically names stormwater from overfertilized lawns, golf courses and agricultural fields.{{cite:noaa_est09}} Some nitrogen also escapes into the air as ammonia and nitrous oxide.{{cite:epa_ag}}
Organisms & processes affected
Fertilizer is designed to make plants grow, and in the water it does the same for algae and phytoplankton.{{cite:cbp_do}}
Ecological consequences
Adds to the nutrient overload described in 5.3 and 5.4; money spent on fertilizer that washes away is also wasted for the farmer.
Real-world example
EPA’s recommended response is “nutrient management”: applying the right amount of fertilizer at the right time, by the right method and in the right place.{{cite:epa_ag}}

5.3

Excess nitrogen and phosphorus

Cause
EPA identifies five main sources: agriculture; stormwater from roofs, roads and parking lots; wastewater from sewer and septic systems that do not remove enough nitrogen and phosphorus; fossil fuels burned in cars and power plants, which release nitrogen into the air; and household detergents, fertilizer and pet waste.{{cite:epa_sources}}
Environmental mechanism
Nitrogen and phosphorus are the nutrients that normally limit plant growth. When they are abundant, algae grow explosively over days to weeks.{{cite:nasa_phyto,cbp_do}}
Organisms & processes affected
Phytoplankton multiply; filter feeders such as oysters and menhaden eat some, but much of the bloom dies and sinks.{{cite:cbp_do}}
Ecological consequences
Murky water, harmful algal blooms and, eventually, low oxygen (5.4).{{cite:epa_ag}}
Real-world example
In 2010 EPA set the Chesapeake Bay Total Maximum Daily Load, a “pollution diet” that limits how much nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment can enter the bay from six states and Washington, D.C.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}

5.4

Eutrophication and hypoxia

Cause
Excess nutrients (5.1 to 5.3) combined with warm, layered summer water.{{cite:cbp_do}}
Environmental mechanism
Algae blooms die and sink, and bacteria that decompose them consume dissolved oxygen until little or none is left. In spring and summer, warm fresh river water floats on cool salty water, and this layering (stratification) stops oxygen at the surface from mixing down.{{cite:cbp_do,cbp_physical}}
Organisms & processes affected
Most estuary animals need at least 5 mg of dissolved oxygen per liter to thrive; bottom fish, crabs and oysters need about 3. Water below 2 mg/L is hypoxic and below 0.2 mg/L is anoxic. Fish and crabs can swim away, but oysters cannot move and are especially vulnerable.{{cite:cbp_do}} In hot summers striped bass are caught in a “habitat squeeze” between surface water that is too warm and deep water without enough oxygen.{{cite:cbp_climate}}
Ecological consequences
Dead zones and fish kills; shaded seagrass beds die back; bottom communities are lost.{{cite:cbp_do,dennison1993}}
Real-world example
The Chesapeake Bay Program describes dead zones in the bay that cause fish kills, and links them to nutrient pollution and to the layering of fresh and salt water that is strongest from spring through summer.{{cite:cbp_do,cbp_physical}}

5.5

Industrial and sewage pollution

Cause
Industrial discharges, street and yard runoff, sewage treatment plants, septic tanks and combined sewer overflows (old sewers that carry rainwater and sewage in the same pipe and overflow into rivers during storms).{{cite:noaa_est09}}
Environmental mechanism
Toxic substances such as pesticides, oil, antifreeze and metals like mercury and lead wash into estuaries and settle into the mud. Some, such as the insecticide DDT and the metal mercury, build up in living tissue and become more concentrated at each step up the food chain, a process called biomagnification.{{cite:noaa_est09}}
Organisms & processes affected
Contaminants pass from algae, shrimp, oysters and fish up to top predators such as ospreys, eagles and people.{{cite:noaa_est09}} Combined sewer overflows are probably the largest source of bacteria and viruses in most estuaries.{{cite:noaa_est09}}
Ecological consequences
Poisoned top predators, unsafe shellfish and swimming water, and sewage nutrients that add to eutrophication (5.4). Osprey populations rebounded after DDT was banned.{{cite:cornell_osprey}}
Real-world example
London’s tidal Thames was declared biologically dead in the late 1950s.{{cite:thameswater_2026}} Its recovery, including a new sewage tunnel, is described in 6.2.

5.6

Plastic pollution

Cause
Litter, bags and bottles carried by storm drains and rivers; plastic resin pellets from manufacturing; and microbeads once added to beauty products.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}}
Environmental mechanism
Plastic does not decay like plant matter. It breaks into smaller and smaller pieces until they are invisible, without losing mass.{{cite:yonkos2014}} Pieces under 5 mm (about the size of a pencil eraser) are called microplastics.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}}
Organisms & processes affected
Aquatic animals and birds can mistake microplastics for food.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}} In an estuary full of filter feeders and plankton-eaters, particles the size of food are a particular concern.
Ecological consequences
NOAA calls microplastics a potential threat to aquatic life, while noting that scientists still know relatively little about their effects.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}}
Real-world example
Researchers sampling four Chesapeake Bay tributaries found microplastics, with the highest concentrations where population density and urban and suburban development were greatest.{{cite:yonkos2014}}

5.7

Coastal development

Cause
Homes, ports, roads and cities built along the shore. Marshes have been drained and filled to create shipping ports and expand urban areas.{{cite:noaa_est09}}
Environmental mechanism
Filling removes marsh outright. Seawalls, bulkheads and jetties “harden” the shoreline: about 14% of the continental U.S. coastline (22,842 km) is armored this way, and armoring is most common where housing is dense.{{cite:gittman2015}} Roofs and pavement also send more stormwater and nutrients into the water.{{cite:epa_sources}}
Organisms & processes affected
Nursery habitat for young fish and crabs, feeding grounds for wading birds, and the marsh’s natural filtering and flood protection.{{cite:noaa_est03}}
Ecological consequences
Shoreline hardening can speed up erosion and the loss of beaches and tidal wetlands, and leaves the marsh nowhere to move as the sea rises.{{cite:gittman2015}}
Real-world example
About 85% of San Francisco Bay’s wetlands (more than 150,000 acres) have been lost to development.{{cite:usfws_sfbay2023}}

5.8

Dredging

Cause
Bottom sediment is dug out to create or maintain waterways deep enough for ships: channels, turning basins, ports and marinas. Because rivers keep delivering sediment, channels must be dredged again and again.{{cite:nmfs_dredge2010}}
Environmental mechanism
Dredging removes or buries animals living in the bottom and stirs up plumes of fine sediment that block light. It can also resuspend toxic metals, pesticides, pathogens and nutrients buried in the mud, expose older, more contaminated layers and briefly lower oxygen.{{cite:nmfs_dredge2010}}
Organisms & processes affected
Worms and small crustaceans that fish eat; eelgrass, whose depth, density and productivity are controlled mainly by light; phytoplankton; and fish, which can suffer higher larval death, reduced feeding and gill injury if sediment stays high.{{cite:nmfs_dredge2010}}
Ecological consequences
Bottom communities in estuarine mud can take from several months to several years to recover, and freshly disturbed bottom is easily colonized by invasive species.{{cite:nmfs_dredge2010}}
Real-world example
In San Francisco Bay, the amount of dredged sediment dumped back into the bay was cut from about 6.0 million cubic yards a year before 1990 to 1.64 million by 2010, with more of it reused, for example in wetland restoration.{{cite:nmfs_dredge2010}}

5.9

Sea-level rise and saltwater intrusion

Cause
Climate change raises sea level. In the Chesapeake Bay, sea-level rise is made worse because the land itself is slowly sinking, a lingering effect of the last ice age.{{cite:cbp_climate,noaa_ghostforest}}
Environmental mechanism
As the sea rises, salt water pushes farther inland and into the fresh groundwater that coastal trees depend on.{{cite:noaa_ghostforest}} Coastal land is converted step by step: as forest dies back, marsh moves in, and the lowest areas can become open water.{{cite:kirwan2019,noaa_ghostforest}}
Organisms & processes affected
Salt slowly poisons coastal trees.{{cite:noaa_ghostforest}} Marshes flooded with salt water can disappear faster than they can move inland.{{cite:cbp_climate}} Warmer water adds stress: eelgrass suffers when water stays above 86 °F for long periods.{{cite:cbp_climate}}
Ecological consequences
Loss of tidal marsh, the habitat and filter at the heart of the estuary, especially where development blocks marshes from migrating inland (5.7).{{cite:cbp_climate,gittman2015}}
Real-world example
NOAA describes “ghost forests” of dead, standing trees along North Carolina’s maritime forests and in Louisiana’s Mississippi Delta, where rising water and sinking land combine.{{cite:noaa_ghostforest}}

5.10

Overfishing

Cause
Harvesting oysters, crabs and fish faster than their populations can replace themselves.{{cite:wilberg2011}}
Environmental mechanism
Removing adults reduces reproduction. Oysters are a special case: their larvae settle on hard surfaces, especially existing shell, so harvesting oysters also removes the reef that the next generation needs.{{cite:cbp_oyster}}
Organisms & processes affected
Oyster reefs and their water filtering; striped bass, for which overfishing is a listed threat; and blue crabs, whose removal can unleash periwinkle grazing on the marsh.{{cite:cbp_stripedbass,silliman2002}}
Ecological consequences
Lost filtering and reef habitat, and trophic cascades: overharvesting blue crabs may have contributed to large salt marsh die-offs in the southeastern United States.{{cite:silliman2002}}
Real-world example
In Maryland’s part of the Chesapeake Bay, oyster numbers have fallen 99.7% since the early 1800s and 92% since 1980, and oyster habitat shrank by about 70% between 1980 and 2009, because of overfishing, disease and habitat loss together.{{cite:wilberg2011}}
