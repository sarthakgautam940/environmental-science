4B Animals: three ways to survive winter

Every boreal animal must get through a winter of deep snow, short days and scarce food. There are three broad strategies:

Resist: stay active

Grow thicker fur, change color, travel on top of snow, hunt cooperatively or live on stored food. Moose (snowshoe hares) lynx, wolves, boreal owls and red squirrels all stay active.

Avoid: go dormant

Shut down until spring. Of the animals on this page, only the brown bear hibernates.{{cite:stenvinkel2013}}

Leave: migrate

Many birds escape the boreal winter entirely. None of the seven animals below is a true seasonal migrant, but some move widely: lynx abandon areas when hares crash, and wolf packs travel over vast distances.{{cite:adfg_lynx,nps_wolves}}

Mammals that stay active share some general winter equipment. In fall they grow extra underfur (short, thick, wavy hairs that trap insulating air beneath longer guard hairs) and muscles in the skin can fluff the fur to trap even more air. Blood vessels in the legs are arranged for countercurrent heat exchange, so warm blood flowing out to the feet heats the cold blood returning to the body.{{cite:nps_yell_winter}} The profiles below focus on the traits that are special to each species.

{{figure:forest/moose|species}}

Moose

Alces alces

Role
Primary consumer; large browsing herbivore
Size
Bulls 900–1,400 lb; cows 700–1,100 lb; 5–6.5 ft at the shoulder
Habitat
Forest near lakes, marshes and willow thickets
Predators
Gray wolves and bears
Ecological role

The moose is the largest member of the deer family and a major browser of willow, shrubs and young trees; it is the main prey of wolves in much of the north.{{cite:nps_moose,adfg_wolf}}

Extremely long legs Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Deep snow buries food and makes walking exhausting; in summer, much good food grows underwater.
Adaptation
Very long legs that hold the body 5 to 6½ feet above the ground.{{cite:nps_moose}}
How it works
Moose have special joints that let them swing their legs up and over the snow instead of plowing through it, as elk must.{{cite:nps_yell_winter}} The same legs let them wade into lakes to pull plants off the bottom and paw through deep snow to reach food.{{cite:nps_moose}}
Why it helps here
In deep snow, moose gather in willow marshes and trample networks of trails called “yards,” saving energy while they feed.{{cite:nps_moose}}
Survival & reproduction
Less energy spent moving means more fat reserves left for late winter and for producing a calf in spring.
Built for cold, stressed by heat Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Winter temperatures drop far below −20 °C while food quality falls.
Adaptation
Exceptional cold tolerance and a lowered winter metabolism. In experiments, moose raised their hair (piloerection) at −25 to −30 °C but did not increase their metabolic rate, and their resting energy use in winter was less than half of that in spring and early summer.{{cite:renecker1986}}
How it works
A huge body has little surface area for its volume, so it loses heat slowly; a dark coat traps an insulating layer of air, and raising the hairs thickens that layer further.{{cite:nps_moose,nps_yell_winter}}
Why it helps here
The flip side is overheating: moose breathe faster when winter air rises above about −5 °C and begin open-mouthed panting at 20 °C in summer.{{cite:renecker1986}} Their design suits a cold climate, and explains why warming is a concern for them.
Survival & reproduction
Spending little energy to stay warm lets moose survive winters on low-quality twig browse.
Switching menus with the seasons Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Leaves and aquatic plants vanish in winter; what remains is twigs and needles, and moose run short of sodium from about September to May.{{cite:belovsky1981}}
Adaptation
A flexible diet: in summer, grasses, forbs, underwater vegetation, shrubs and leaves; in winter, woody browse such as willow and balsam fir.{{cite:nps_moose,feis_abibal}}
How it works
Balsam fir twigs weigh 8–13 times more than deciduous twigs of similar length, so a moose can gather more food with less time and energy when snow is deep.{{cite:feis_abibal}} Researchers studying Isle Royale moose proposed that they store sodium by replacing it with potassium in their saliva and rumen fluids during the months of shortage.{{cite:belovsky1981}}
Why it helps here
Feeding efficiently on whatever the season offers makes the most of a short growing season.
Survival & reproduction
Well-fed cows are more likely to raise calves.
{{figure:forest/snowshoe-hare-winter|species}}

Snowshoe hare

Lepus americanus

Role
Primary consumer; the forest’s central prey species
Size
1.4–1.8 kg (3–4 lb); 46–51 cm long
Diet
Summer: grasses, buds, leaves. Winter: spruce twigs and needles, bark, aspen and willow buds
Young
2–3 litters a year
Ecological role

Snowshoe hares convert twigs and bark into food for nearly every predator in the forest, and their famous ten-year population cycle (first noticed in Hudson’s Bay Company fur-trade records) ripples through the whole food web.{{cite:adfg_hare,krebs2001}}

A coat that changes color with the seasons Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Lynx, owls and other predators hunt by sight, and the background switches from brown forest floor to white snow and back each year.
Adaptation
Two molts a year: a yellowish- to grayish-brown summer coat is replaced by a white winter coat, whose hairs stay dusky at the base over gray underfur.{{cite:adfg_hare}}
How it works
Across animals that change color seasonally, the main cue that times the molt is photoperiod (day length) acting through hormone (neuroendocrine) signals that control hair growth.{{cite:zimova2018}} As autumn days shorten, follicles grow new hairs without dark pigment; lengthening days in spring trigger brown hairs again. White hairs contain air instead of pigment, which may also improve insulation.{{cite:nps_yell_winter}}
Why it helps here
The main function of the seasonal color change is camouflage against snow.{{cite:zimova2018}} A hare that matches its background is harder for visual predators to find.
Survival & reproduction
Hares that survive winter breed from mid-May through August.{{cite:adfg_hare}} But because day length, not snow, sets the timing, a warming climate creates mismatch: hares could not shift when their color change starts, and models forecast four to eight times more days of white hares on brown ground by 2100.{{cite:mills2013}}
{{figure:forest/snowshoe-hare-summer|inline}}
The “snowshoe” hind feet Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Fleeing a predator through soft, deep snow.
Adaptation
Large, heavily furred hind feet, hence the name “snowshoe.”{{cite:adfg_hare}}
How it works
Pressure equals weight divided by area. Spreading the hare’s weight over large, furry feet lowers the pressure on the snow, so the hare sinks less.{{cite:nps_yell_winter}}
Why it helps here
On soft snow a hare can bound away from predators that sink; in winter it also follows a network of packed trails, often along its summer pathways.{{cite:adfg_hare}}
Survival & reproduction
Escaping predators through winter is what lets a hare live to breed in spring.
Fast breeding Life-history adaptation
Environmental challenge
Heavy predation and periodic population crashes.
Adaptation
Hares breed at about one year old and have two to three litters a year; females mate again right after giving birth.{{cite:adfg_hare}}
How it works
Pregnancy lasts only 36–37 days, and the young (leverets) are born fully furred with eyes open, able to walk as soon as their fur is dry and eating plants within two weeks.{{cite:adfg_hare}}
Why it helps here
Precocial young need little care, so a mother can raise several litters within one short summer.
Survival & reproduction
Populations can rebuild quickly after a crash, one reason the hare cycle repeats.
{{figure:forest/canada-lynx|species}}

Canada lynx

Lynx canadensis

Role
Secondary consumer; specialist predator of hares
Look
Short tail, long legs, furry feet, long black ear tufts
Main prey
Snowshoe hare; also grouse, rodents
Young
Usually 2–4 kittens
Ecological role

The lynx is a specialist predator of snowshoe hares. Its numbers rise and fall with the hare cycle, lagging one to two years behind.{{cite:adfg_lynx,krebs2001}}

Snowshoe paws Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Chasing hares across deep, soft snow.
Adaptation
Large, broad feet that “function as snowshoes,” with extra fur growing between the toes in winter.{{cite:adfg_lynx,nps_yell_winter}}
How it works
Foot-load is body mass divided by foot area. Lynx and coyotes are similar in size, but in a Yukon study coyotes had 4.1 to 8.8 times the foot-load of lynx and sank deeper into the snow.{{cite:murray1991}}
Why it helps here
Where snow is deep and soft, the lynx can stay on top while competing predators flounder or must stick to packed trails.{{cite:murray1991}}
Survival & reproduction
Hunting success in winter determines whether a lynx survives and whether females produce kittens.
A life tied to the hare cycle Life-history adaptation
Environmental challenge
Snowshoe hares go through an 8–11-year cycle of abundance and scarcity.{{cite:adfg_lynx}}
Adaptation
Reproduction that tracks the food supply, plus the ability to switch prey or move away.
How it works
When hares are plentiful, a high percentage of females one year or older produce kittens, and most survive. When hares are scarce, very few yearlings breed and very few kittens survive to winter. Lynx then turn to grouse and rodents (occasionally even caribou, Dall sheep or foxes) or leave the area.{{cite:adfg_lynx}}
Why it helps here
Matching the number of young to the food available avoids wasting energy on kittens that cannot be fed.
Survival & reproduction
Kittens stay with their mother until late winter, learning the hunting skills they need to survive.{{cite:adfg_lynx}}
Ecological relationship

The hare–lynx cycle

In 1831, a Hudson’s Bay Company post manager in northern Ontario reported that local Ojibway people were starving because “rabbits” (snowshoe hares) had become scarce. Such shortages recurred roughly every ten years, and when biologists plotted the company’s fur-trade records in the early 1900s, lynx numbers rose and fell in step with hares.{{cite:krebs2001}} Long-term studies in the Yukon concluded that the cycle is driven by the interaction of hares with both their food and their predators.{{cite:adfg_lynx,krebs2001}}

{{figure:forest/gray-wolf|species}}

Gray wolf

Canis lupus

Role
Tertiary consumer; apex predator
Size
Interior Alaska males 38–52 kg (85–115 lb)
Social unit
Packs averaging 6–7; sometimes 20–30
Main prey
Moose and caribou; also sheep, hares, beaver
Ecological role

Wolves are the top predators of large boreal herbivores. In most of mainland Alaska, moose and/or caribou are their primary food, supplemented by Dall sheep, squirrels (snowshoe hares) beaver and occasionally birds and fish; they also scavenge.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}

Cooperative pack hunting Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
The main prey, the moose, can weigh ten times as much as a wolf and is dangerous to attack.{{cite:nps_moose,adfg_wolf}}
Adaptation
Living and hunting in packs, usually a breeding pair and their offspring, that defend a shared territory.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
How it works
Working together, a pack can bring down prey no single wolf could. In winter a pack may kill a deer or moose every few days.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
Why it helps here
Large herbivores are the most abundant food in the boreal winter, when small prey hide under snow.
Survival & reproduction
Shared kills feed the pups, typically one litter of about seven a year per pack.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
Testing herds for the weakest animal Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Healthy adult moose can outrun or fight off wolves.
Adaptation
Opportunistic hunting focused on young, old or weakened animals.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
How it works
Wolves select the prey that is easiest to catch. When snow is unusually deep, even prime-age animals become vulnerable.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
Why it helps here
Deep snow slows and exhausts large hoofed animals, shifting the odds toward the pack.{{cite:adfg_wolf}}
Survival & reproduction
Less risky hunts mean fewer injuries for the hunters, and predation that removes weak animals from prey herds.
Feast, famine and long-distance travel Physiological and behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Prey is spread thinly across a vast, cold landscape.
Adaptation
Wolves can go several days with almost no food, and packs travel over vast expanses of terrain to hunt.{{cite:adfg_wolf,nps_wolves}}
How it works
A large kill is eaten quickly, then the pack lives on stored energy while it searches. A thick winter underfur beneath long guard hairs keeps them warm on the move.{{cite:nps_yell_winter}}
Why it helps here
In a land of scattered prey, the ability to cover ground and endure hunger matters more than speed.
Survival & reproduction
Packs that hold large territories can find enough prey to raise pups year after year.
{{figure:forest/brown-bear|species}}

Brown bear (grizzly)

Ursus arctos

Role
Omnivore; top predator and scavenger
Range
Boreal and mountain forests of North America, Scandinavia and Russia
Diet
Omnivore: plants, berries, fish, clams, small mammals
Winter
Hibernates 5–6 months; more than 7 in the Arctic
Ecological role

Brown bears are omnivores, eating a mixed diet of plants, berries, fish and small mammals, and they also prey on vulnerable large animals such as moose calves and elk calves.{{cite:nps_brownbear,nps_moose}} As apex predators, their condition reflects the health of the ecosystems they live in.{{cite:nps_arctic_bears}} In Katmai, Alaska, they dig for clams and catch sockeye salmon all summer before entering dens in mid-October to November.{{cite:nps_katm}}

Hibernation: five to seven months without eating or drinking Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
For half the year, the plants, fish and other foods a bear depends on are unavailable under snow and ice.
Adaptation
Hibernation. Brown bears hibernate for five to six months each winter, eating and drinking nothing and producing no urine; Arctic brown bears in Alaska averaged 206 days in their dens, and one stayed 233 days, 64% of the year.{{cite:stenvinkel2013,nps_arctic_bears}}
How it works
Activity, heart rate and body temperature start to fall weeks before den entry, and bears go into dens when snow arrives and air temperature reaches about 0 °C; inside, their metabolism is strongly suppressed.{{cite:evans2016}} The body lives on stored fat while recycling the nitrogen in urea back into proteins, which protects muscle and bone from wasting.{{cite:stenvinkel2013}}
Why it helps here
Sleeping through the foodless season costs far less energy than searching for food in deep snow.
Survival & reproduction
Bears emerge in spring with muscles and bones intact. Females with cubs stay in their dens longer, and pregnant females choose higher, snowier den sites.{{cite:nps_arctic_bears}}
Fattening up before winter Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
A bear must store all the energy for winter during a short summer and fall.
Adaptation
Intense late-season feeding and flexible timing of den entry.
How it works
Bears build large fat stores before denning; grizzlies in Yellowstone spend most of their time feeding, eating up to 30 pounds of food a day to store fat for winter.{{cite:stenvinkel2013,nps_brownbear}} When food is plentiful in the fall, Arctic brown bears delay entering their dens to keep feeding.{{cite:nps_arctic_bears}}
Why it helps here
Every extra week of feeding adds fat that must last through a winter that may stretch past seven months.
Survival & reproduction
Fat reserves fuel both survival and, for females, nursing cubs in the den.
Long claws and a digging shoulder Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Much of the food in a cold, short-summer forest is buried or seasonal: roots, bulbs, burrowing rodents, clams.
Adaptation
Long, strong claws and powerful shoulders.{{cite:nps_brownbear}}
How it works
Brown bears use their claws to dig for food, pick fruits and catch prey; the strong shoulder muscles make them efficient diggers.{{cite:nps_brownbear}}
Why it helps here
Being able to dig lets a bear switch among roots, bulbs, berries, fish and small mammals as each becomes available.{{cite:nps_brownbear}}
Survival & reproduction
A broad, flexible diet helps bears reach the fat reserves they need to survive hibernation.
Common misconception: Not every boreal mammal hibernates. Of the animals profiled here, only the brown bear does; moose, hares, lynx, wolves and red squirrels stay active all winter.
{{figure:forest/boreal-owl|species}}

Boreal owl

Aegolius funereus (Tengmalm’s owl in Europe)

Role
Secondary consumer; nocturnal predator of small mammals
Habitat
Spruce, fir, aspen, poplar and birch stands across the boreal forest
Diet
Voles, mice, shrews, squirrels; some birds and insects
Nest
Old woodpecker holes and natural cavities; 4–6 eggs
Ecological role

A small owl that hunts at night, the boreal owl preys on voles and other small mammals and depends on woodpeckers for nest holes.{{cite:cornell_borealowl,mndnr_borealowl}}

Lopsided ears for hunting in the dark Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Long winter nights, and prey that moves hidden under vegetation and snow.
Adaptation
Asymmetrical ears. The ear openings in the skull are offset: the right one sits about 6.5 mm higher than the left, so a line between them tilts about 12° from horizontal.{{cite:norberg1978}}
How it works
A sound from below reaches the two ears at slightly different loudness and timing. By comparing the two signals, the owl’s brain calculates not just left-to-right direction but also height, “excellent physical cues to vertical localization of sound.” A large air-filled middle ear also improves detection of the low-frequency rustling made by prey moving through vegetation.{{cite:norberg1978}}
Why it helps here
Boreal owls sit on a perch listening and dive when they hear something scurrying below; in winter they hunt in spruce–fir stands where uncrusted snow lets them reach prey.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl,cornell_borealowl}}
Survival & reproduction
Precise hearing lets the owl catch food in near-total darkness through the long northern winter.
Caching prey and thawing it Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Hunting success varies from night to night, and some nights bring nothing.
Adaptation
Food storage: boreal owls sometimes store extra food in cold weather, then thaw it later by sitting on it.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl}}
How it works
Surplus prey is stored and may freeze solid in the cold; the owl later warms it with its own body heat by sitting on it.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl}}
Why it helps here
A long-term study of breeding Tengmalm’s (boreal) owls found that prey caching acts as a buffer against temporary food shortages.{{cite:korpimaki1987}}
Survival & reproduction
Stored food helps keep the female and chicks fed during bad hunting spells.
Nesting in tree cavities Reproductive and behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Eggs and chicks must stay warm and hidden from predators such as martens and larger owls.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl}}
Adaptation
Nesting in holes made by woodpeckers or in natural cavities; the male advertises several possible sites and the female chooses.{{cite:cornell_borealowl}}
How it works
The female lays four to six eggs and does most of the incubation while the male hunts and brings her food; eggs hatch in about 27–28 days.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl}}
Why it helps here
A wooden cavity shelters the nest from wind and cold and is hard for predators to reach.
Survival & reproduction
Young fledge after about 28–36 days.{{cite:mndnr_borealowl}} Because the owls need old trees with woodpecker holes, they depend on mature forest.
{{figure:forest/red-squirrel|species}}

American red squirrel

Tamiasciurus hudsonicus

Role
Primary consumer; seed predator and food hoarder
Habitat
Conifer forests across boreal North America
Main food
Seeds from white spruce and other conifer cones
Behavior
Territorial; defends a central food store
Ecological role

In many boreal regions, red squirrels are the main vertebrate predator of white spruce seed crops.{{cite:fletcher2010}} In turn, squirrels are prey for predators such as the boreal owl.{{cite:cornell_borealowl}}

Larder hoarding in middens Behavioral adaptation
Environmental challenge
Fresh food all but disappears for six months, and cones release their seeds soon after ripening.
Adaptation
Larder hoarding: squirrels cut green, unopened cones in late summer and fall and pack them into a central store called a midden. One squirrel may collect more than 15,000 cones.{{cite:northernwoodlands_midden}}
How it works
Cones are buried in the cool, moist midden, which keeps them from drying out and opening; the squirrel later digs them out and drops the inedible cone scales back on the pile.{{cite:northernwoodlands_midden}} By hoarding unopened cones underground, squirrels interrupt the normal sequence of cone opening, seed dispersal and germination.{{cite:fletcher2010}}
Why it helps here
A squirrel that relies on a stored food supply is not limited by how much fat it can carry on its body.{{cite:fletcher2010}}
Survival & reproduction
A large midden can hold enough food for more than one winter, worth defending, since squirrels steal from each other, and bears sometimes raid middens too.{{cite:northernwoodlands_midden}}
{{figure:forest/squirrel-midden|inline}}
Breeding in anticipation of a bumper crop Reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
White spruce produces huge seed crops only once every several years (see white spruce).{{cite:feis_picgla}}
Adaptation
Anticipatory reproduction: red squirrels (and Eurasian tree squirrels) increase their reproductive output before a mast year.{{cite:boutin2006}}
How it works
Females raise more young in the breeding season before the big seed crop is available, so the population is already growing when the food pulse arrives.{{cite:boutin2006}}
Why it helps here
Most seed predators can only respond after food appears; anticipating the pulse eliminates that lag.{{cite:boutin2006}}
Survival & reproduction
Populations grow in step with the food supply, so more young survive their first winter.

Questions to be ready for

Likely questions about forest adaptations, with short answers. Click a question to reveal the answer.

Why are most boreal trees evergreen when the winters are so harsh?

The growing season is only about four months, and soils are poor. Keeping needles lets a conifer photosynthesize as soon as spring conditions allow, continue into fall, and avoid spending scarce nutrients on a new set of leaves every year.{{cite:saupe_conifers}}

How do needles reduce water loss in winter?

They have little surface area, a waxy cuticle, and stomata recessed in sheltered chambers. This matters because frozen soil makes winter a drought for trees.{{cite:saupe_conifers}}

Why is a conical shape useful?

A single leader and sloping branches shed snow, and flexible branches bend rather than break under heavy loads.{{cite:saupe_conifers}}

How do serotinous cones work, and which species has them?

Lodgepole pine cones are sealed by resin that melts at about 45–50 °C during fire, releasing seed onto burned ground. Black spruce has semi-serotinous cones that also release seed after fire.{{cite:feis_pinconl,feis_picmar}}

Is tamarack an evergreen?

No. Tamarack is a deciduous conifer: its needles turn gold and fall each autumn, partly as an adaptation to a very cold climate.{{cite:bc_tamarack}}

What triggers the snowshoe hare’s color change?

Mainly day length (photoperiod), acting through hormones that control hair growth, not snow. That is why hares can end up white on brown ground when snow comes late or melts early.{{cite:zimova2018,mills2013}}

How do the lynx’s paws help it hunt?

Large, furry paws spread its weight over a big area, lowering foot-load so it sinks less. Coyotes of similar size have 4.1–8.8 times the foot-load.{{cite:murray1991}}

Which animals hibernate?

Of the species on this page, only the brown bear. It goes five to six months (up to seven in the Arctic) without eating, drinking or urinating, recycling urea to protect its muscles.{{cite:stenvinkel2013,nps_arctic_bears}}

How does the boreal owl find prey it cannot see?

Its ear openings are at different heights, so it can compare the sound reaching each ear to pinpoint both direction and height of rustling prey.{{cite:norberg1978}}

How is a moose adapted to deep snow and cold?

Very long legs and special joints let it step over snow, and its insulation is so good it did not raise its metabolism even at −30 °C, though it overheats easily in warm weather.{{cite:nps_moose,nps_yell_winter,renecker1986}}

Why do red squirrels store cones instead of seeds?

Unopened green cones kept in a cool, moist midden do not dry out and open, so the seeds stay inside until the squirrel needs them.{{cite:northernwoodlands_midden}}
