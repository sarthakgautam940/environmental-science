6

Potential solutions

Because an estuary is fed by its whole watershed, solutions work at every scale: on farms and in sewers far upstream, along the shoreline, and in the water itself. Each solution lists the problems from section 5 that it addresses.

6.1

Agricultural runoff reduction

Addresses 5.1 5.2 5.3 5.4

How it works
Farm practices keep nutrients and soil on the field and out of the water: nutrient management (the right amount of fertilizer, at the right time, in the right place), cover crops and perennial plants that keep the ground covered all year, planted buffers along field edges and streams that absorb and filter nutrients, conservation tillage, woodchip bioreactors that treat drainage water, and fences that keep livestock out of streams.{{cite:epa_ag}}
Problem it addresses
Cuts the nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment that start the chain leading to algae blooms and dead zones.
Real-world example
Under the Chesapeake Bay Total Maximum Daily Load set in 2010, six states and Washington, D.C. wrote Watershed Implementation Plans and two-year milestones to reduce nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment, with practices for 60% of the reductions in place by 2017 and all of them by 2025.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}
Limits & trade-offs
Depends on the cooperation of thousands of individual farms and landowners, and practices cost time and money to install and maintain.

6.2

Better wastewater treatment

Addresses 5.3 5.4 5.5

How it works
Upgraded treatment plants remove more nitrogen and phosphorus before water is released, since ordinary sewer and septic systems do not always remove enough.{{cite:epa_sources}} Storage tunnels catch the mix of rain and sewage that old combined sewers used to spill into rivers during storms, and send it for treatment instead.{{cite:thameswater_tunnel}}
Problem it addresses
Reduces nutrients feeding algae blooms, and the bacteria, viruses and sewage litter that make water unsafe.
Real-world example
London’s Thames Tideway Tunnel, a 25-km sewer running mostly beneath the river, cost about £4.6 billion.{{cite:thameswater_tunnel}} In its first full year, storm overflows into the tidal Thames fell by about 86% compared with the average before the tunnel (from more than 21 million to just under 3 million cubic meters), and about 84 tonnes of sewage-derived litter were kept out of the river.{{cite:thameswater_2026}} 2025 was an unusually dry year, but the operator reports that the reduction was much larger than the weather alone would explain.{{cite:thameswater_2026}}
Limits & trade-offs
Very expensive and slow to build; it does not stop nutrients that arrive as farm or street runoff.

6.3

Wetland and marsh restoration

Addresses 5.7 5.8 5.9 5.3

How it works
Levees around diked land are breached to let the tide back in. Sediment settles, cordgrass and other marsh plants recolonize, and the marsh again filters water and absorbs floods.{{cite:usfws_sfbay2023,noaa_est03}} Smooth cordgrass is planted to stabilize tidal mudflats and dredged-material sites in marsh restoration projects.{{cite:usda_spal}}
Problem it addresses
Replaces marsh lost to development and filling, gives clean dredged sediment a useful home, and creates room for marshes to keep up with rising seas.{{cite:cbp_climate}}
Real-world example
The South Bay Salt Pond Restoration Project is the largest tidal wetland restoration on the U.S. West Coast and will restore 15,100 acres of former industrial salt ponds in San Francisco Bay.{{cite:southbay}} In December 2023 a 300-acre former salt pond was opened to the tides, part of a 50-year effort.{{cite:usfws_sfbay2023}}
Limits & trade-offs
Restored marshes take years to mature, and they need enough sediment to build up as fast as the sea rises.

6.4

Living shorelines

Addresses 5.7 5.9

How it works
Instead of a concrete seawall, the shore is stabilized with natural materials such as marsh plants, sand, rock and oyster shell. Plants and reefs absorb wave energy, store floodwater, filter runoff and store carbon, and they become more stable over time as roots grow.{{cite:noaa_livingshorelines}}
Problem it addresses
Protects property without the habitat loss and erosion that come with hardened shorelines.{{cite:gittman2015}}
Real-world example
NOAA reports that during major storms living shorelines perform better than hardened shorelines, and that natural habitats support more fish.{{cite:noaa_livingshorelines}}
Limits & trade-offs
Not suited to every site; very exposed, high-energy shores may still need some hard structure, such as the low rock sill shown below.

6.5

Pollution prevention

Addresses 5.2 5.5 5.6

How it works
Stopping pollutants before they reach the water is cheaper than removing them afterward. At home this means using less yard fertilizer, cleaning up pet waste and choosing low-phosphorus detergents.{{cite:epa_sources}} For plastics, it means keeping litter out of storm drains, since microplastics come largely from larger debris breaking apart.{{cite:noaa_microplastics}} For toxic chemicals, it means banning or restricting the most persistent ones.
Problem it addresses
Reduces fertilizer, toxic chemicals and plastic entering the estuary at their sources.
Real-world example
After DDT was banned, osprey populations rebounded, helped by nesting platforms.{{cite:cornell_osprey}} The Chesapeake Bay, historically home to the world’s largest concentration of breeding ospreys, now supports an estimated 8,000 to 10,000 breeding pairs.{{cite:cbp_osprey}}
Limits & trade-offs
Relies on millions of individual choices; chemicals already buried in sediment remain for decades.

6.6

Sustainable fishing and oyster restoration

Addresses 5.10 5.4

How it works
Catch limits and seasons keep harvest below what a population can replace; sanctuaries protect breeding stock. For oysters, restoration builds new reefs so that larvae have hard surfaces to settle on.{{cite:cbp_oyster,noaa_oyster2020}}
Problem it addresses
Rebuilds overharvested populations and the filtering and habitat that oyster reefs provide.
Real-world example
By 2020, about 358 acres of oyster reef (an area larger than the National Mall) had been restored in Maryland’s Little Choptank River, beating the previous record of 348 acres in nearby Harris Creek, part of a goal to restore reefs in 10 Chesapeake tributaries by 2025.{{cite:noaa_oyster2020}}
Limits & trade-offs
Disease and low oxygen can still kill restored oysters, and fishing limits can be hard on watermen who depend on the harvest.{{cite:cbp_oyster,cbp_do}}

6.7

Habitat protection

Addresses 5.7 5.8 5.9

How it works
Legally protecting marshes, seagrass beds and the undeveloped land behind them keeps habitat intact and leaves room for marshes to move inland as the sea rises.{{cite:cbp_climate}}
Problem it addresses
Prevents habitat loss before it happens, which is far cheaper than restoring it later.
Real-world example
The National Estuarine Research Reserve System, a partnership between NOAA and coastal states created under the Coastal Zone Management Act, protects about 1.4 million acres at 30 coastal sites.{{cite:noaa_nerrs}}
Limits & trade-offs
Protected areas cover only part of the coast, and they cannot stop pollution that flows in from upstream.

6.8

Estuarine monitoring and conservation programs

Addresses all of 5.1 to 5.10

How it works
Long-term measurements of salinity, oxygen, nutrients and wildlife show whether conditions are getting better or worse, and whether solutions are working. Programs also coordinate the work of federal, state and local partners.{{cite:noaa_nerrs,epa_nep}}
Problem it addresses
Turns scattered efforts into a coordinated plan with measurable goals.
Real-world example
EPA’s National Estuary Program works to improve the water, habitats and living resources of 28 estuaries of national significance.{{cite:epa_nep}} The research reserves run research, monitoring, training and education programs.{{cite:noaa_nerrs}} In the Chesapeake, EPA assessed the 2025 results of the bay’s pollution diet.{{cite:epa_tmdl}}
Limits & trade-offs
Monitoring shows problems but cannot fix them by itself; programs depend on steady funding over decades.
