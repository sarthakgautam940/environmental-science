4A Producers: living with salt, flooding and airless mud

Salt: exclude, store or secrete

Halophytes, salt-tolerant plants, survive by controlling how much sodium and chloride they take up, locking excess salt away in cell vacuoles and making harmless “compatible” solutes to balance it; some also have salt glands.{{cite:flowers2008}}

Oxygen for drowned roots

Marsh soil is waterlogged and nearly oxygen-free, and it accumulates hydrogen sulfide, which poisons roots’ energy production.{{cite:noaa_est06,koch1990}} Plants that live there pipe air to their roots through spongy tissue called aerenchyma.{{cite:usda_spal}}

Flooding and shifting sediment

Tides submerge low marsh twice a day and drop or scour sediment. Creeping underground stems (rhizomes) anchor plants and let them spread without seed.{{cite:cbp_cordgrass,usda_spal}}

Light in murky water

Underwater plants need clear water; the health of submerged grasses is so closely tied to water clarity that scientists use them to assess water quality.{{cite:dennison1993}}

Smooth cordgrass

Spartina alterniflora (also listed as Sporobolus alterniflorus)

Role
Primary producer; foundation species of the low marsh
Habitat
Low marsh, from the water’s edge up; flooded every high tide
Size
Short form to 2 ft; tall form to 7 ft along creek banks
Chesapeake status
The dominant grass of the Bay’s salt marshes
Ecological role

Smooth cordgrass dominates the low marsh down to the estuary’s edge and is one of the main components of marsh peat.{{cite:noaa_est06}} It is the dominant grass of Chesapeake Bay’s salt marshes and provides habitat for marsh periwinkles, ribbed mussels and fiddler crabs.{{cite:cbp_cordgrass}} Its dead leaves become detritus that feeds the marsh food web.

Filtering, storing and secreting salt Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
Roots sit in salty water every day. Salt pulls water out of plant cells and is toxic inside them.
Adaptation
A three-part salt strategy: roots that filter salt out of the water the plant absorbs, leaves that expel excess salt, and cells that tolerate what remains.{{cite:noaa_est07,bradley1991}}
How it works
Membranes in the roots exclude much of the sodium and chloride. Salt that does enter is moved to the leaves, where salt glands secrete it onto the leaf surface. Inside cells, salt that remains is stored in vacuoles while the cytoplasm builds up harmless “compatible” solutes; together they keep the cells’ internal concentration high enough to keep drawing water from salty soil.{{cite:noaa_est07,bradley1991,flowers2008}}
Why it helps here
Most plants die in seawater-soaked soil; few competitors can live in the low marsh.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
Survival & reproduction
Cordgrass forms dense, nearly single-species stands across the zone flooded every day.
Aerenchyma: air pipes to the roots Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Waterlogged marsh peat contains almost no oxygen, and it accumulates hydrogen sulfide, which blocks roots’ ability to make energy and absorb nitrogen.{{cite:noaa_est06,koch1990}}
Adaptation
Spongy tissue with large air spaces (aerenchyma), hollow stems and long hollow rhizomes.{{cite:usda_spal,cbp_cordgrass}}
How it works
The air spaces form a continuous pipeline from the leaves above water down to the buried roots, so oxygen can move to tissues that are surrounded by airless mud. Cordgrass grows significantly more aerenchyma in its roots when flooded, which also reduces the roots’ oxygen needs.{{cite:usda_spal}}
Why it helps here
Roots must respire; without an internal air supply they would suffocate in the low marsh.
Survival & reproduction
Cordgrass can colonize mud flooded twice a day, territory no other marsh grass can hold.
Rhizomes that grip shifting mud Reproductive and structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Tides and storms deposit and scour sediment; seedlings are easily washed away.
Adaptation
Spreading mainly by long underground rhizomes that send up new stems, with a strong, interconnected root system.{{cite:cbp_cordgrass,usda_spal}}
How it works
One plant becomes a large connected colony that shares resources; the root mat binds loose mud. Flowers bloom in July–September and mature into foot-long seed spikes, so it can also colonize new sites by seed.{{cite:cbp_cordgrass}}
Why it helps here
Anchored colonies resist erosion; the U.S. Department of Agriculture uses smooth cordgrass to control shoreline erosion and stabilize tidal mudflats and dredge-fill sites in marsh restoration.{{cite:usda_spal}}
Survival & reproduction
As stems trap sediment and roots add peat, the marsh builds itself up over time.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
Exam tip: Cordgrass has animal partners. Fiddler crab burrows aerate the soil, and ribbed mussels fertilize it; experiments show both increase cordgrass growth.{{cite:bertness1985,bertness1984}}

Eelgrass

Zostera marina

Role
Primary producer; submerged flowering plant
Habitat
Shallow, sandy, medium- to high-salinity water
Leaves
Ribbon-like, up to 4 ft long
Chesapeake range
Saltier middle to lower Bay only
Ecological role

Eelgrass is a true flowering plant that lives entirely underwater. Its beds are key foraging and hiding places for blue crabs, juvenile and molting crabs shelter among the leaves, and food for waterfowl such as brant and redheads and for green turtles.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}

Pollination underwater Reproductive adaptation
Environmental challenge
Flowers cannot rely on wind or insects beneath the surface.
Adaptation
Water pollination and floating seed shoots.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}
How it works
In early summer, female flowers are fertilized by pollen drifting in the water. The fertilized flowers develop into seed-bearing shoots that break off, float to the surface and release their seeds.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}
Why it helps here
Currents and tides that would be a problem for land flowers become the delivery system for pollen and seed.
Survival & reproduction
Floating shoots carry seeds to new shallow areas, starting new beds.
Ribbon leaves and creeping rhizomes Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Tidal currents tug at plants rooted in loose sand and mud.
Adaptation
Long, flexible, ribbon-like leaves with a tube-shaped sheath at the base, and thick creeping rhizomes with many roots. (Zostera means “belt.”){{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}
How it works
Flexible leaves bend with moving water instead of resisting it, while rhizomes anchor the plant and spread it sideways; new plants also grow from rhizomes and from winter buds.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}
Why it helps here
Dense, rooted beds hold sediment in place and create the sheltered, three-dimensional habitat young crabs need.{{cite:cbp_eelgrass}}
Survival & reproduction
Clonal spread lets a bed persist and grow even when seed production is poor.
Why it matters: Because eelgrass needs clear water and is stressed when water stays warmer than 86 °F for long periods, it is a sensitive indicator of pollution and climate change.{{cite:dennison1993,cbp_climate}}

Phytoplankton

Diatoms (class Bacillariophyceae, such as Paralia sulcata, pictured), dinoflagellates (Dinophyceae), cyanobacteria and other microscopic algae

Role
Primary producer; base of the open-water food web
Size
Microscopic, mostly single cells
Lifespan
Individuals rarely live more than a few days
Examples
Silica-encased diatoms, dinoflagellates
Ecological role

Phytoplankton are the foundation of the aquatic food web, feeding everything from microscopic zooplankton to whales; like land plants they use chlorophyll to capture sunlight, consuming carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen.{{cite:nasa_phyto}} In estuaries they are food for filter feeders such as oysters and menhaden.{{cite:cbp_oyster,cbp_do}}

Explosive growth: blooms Life-history adaptation
Environmental challenge
Nutrients and light arrive in pulses, after spring runoff, after storms, when layers mix in fall.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
Adaptation
Very short generation times: when conditions are right, populations can grow explosively over a few days or weeks.{{cite:nasa_phyto}}
How it works
A single cell divides into two, then four, then eight. Because each individual lives only a few days, the population turns over constantly and responds almost immediately to new nutrients.{{cite:nasa_phyto}}
Why it helps here
Estuaries receive nutrient pulses from the whole watershed; rapid growth lets phytoplankton capture them before anything else can.
Survival & reproduction
Blooms feed the whole food web, but too many nutrients cause blooms so large that their decay strips oxygen from the water (see 5.4).{{cite:cbp_do}}
Drifting in the sunlit surface water Structural adaptation
Environmental challenge
Estuary water is often murky, so light fades quickly with depth.{{cite:schubel1968}}
Adaptation
Tiny, free-floating cells with chlorophyll; some types are encased in glassy silica shells (diatoms) and others are armored or swimming forms.{{cite:nasa_phyto}}
How it works
Small cells have a large surface area for their volume, so they absorb nutrients efficiently and sink slowly, staying in the sunlit upper layer where photosynthesis is possible.
Why it helps here
Phytoplankton travel with the water itself, riding the estuary’s circulation instead of needing roots or sediment.
Survival & reproduction
Cells that stay in the light keep dividing.

Glasswort and salt hay

Salicornia spp. (glasswort, pickleweed), Spartina patens (saltmeadow cordgrass)

Role
Primary producers; salt-tolerant high-marsh vegetation
Habitat
High marsh and salt pannes, flooded only by the highest tides
Glasswort
Edible succulent with jointed, fleshy stems
Salt hay
Very fine-leaved grass, 1–2 ft tall
Ecological role

Above the cordgrass zone, the high marsh floods only about twice a month. Salt hay and spike grass dominate it, black rush grows at the highest elevations, and glasswort grows in salt pannes, shallow depressions where seawater evaporates and leaves salt behind.{{cite:noaa_est06}}

{{figure:estuary/high-marsh|inline}}
Glasswort: succulence and salt storage Physiological adaptation
Environmental challenge
In salt pannes, evaporation concentrates salt over many tidal cycles; glasswort is one of the only organisms that can survive there.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
Adaptation
A succulent, salt-loving (halophytic) plant with fleshy stems instead of thin leaves.{{cite:noaa_est07}}
How it works
Halophytes control salt uptake and pack sodium and chloride into the large central vacuoles of their cells, while the surrounding cytoplasm accumulates compatible solutes.{{cite:flowers2008}} Swollen, water-filled cells dilute the stored salt, keeping it away from enzymes.
Why it helps here
Storing salt raises the cells’ internal concentration, so they can still absorb water from extremely salty soil.
Survival & reproduction
Glasswort occupies the saltiest ground in the marsh, where it faces almost no competition.
Living at the right height Ecological pattern
Environmental challenge
Just a few centimeters of elevation change how often a spot floods and how salty it gets.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
Adaptation
Each species has its own salt and flooding tolerance, producing distinct bands (zonation): smooth cordgrass in the low marsh; salt hay and spike grass in the high marsh; black rush highest.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
How it works
Plants that tolerate more flooding and salt grow lower; less tolerant plants grow higher. Fine-leaved salt hay forms dense mats on the rarely flooded high marsh.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
Why it helps here
Matching tolerance to elevation lets each species grow where it does best.
Survival & reproduction
Zonation packs many plant communities, and the animals that depend on them, into a narrow strip of shoreline.
