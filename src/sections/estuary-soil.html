Bottom substrate

An estuary’s “soil” is the sediment on its bottom and in its marshes: fine, nutrient-rich and constantly delivered by rivers and tides, but starved of oxygen just below the surface.

How rivers and tides move and drop sediment

Rivers deliver mud and sand from the watershed, and tides carry sediment back and forth. Water movements in estuaries transport organisms, nutrients, oxygen, sediments and wastes.{{cite:noaa_est05}} At Elkhorn Slough in California, strong tidal currents scour the wetlands and carry so much sediment out on each low tide that a muddy plume reaches a mile or more into Monterey Bay.{{cite:noaa_est01}}

Where fresh and salt water meet, sediment is trapped. Tiny clay plates repel one another in fresh water, but the sodium and chloride ions in salt water neutralize those forces, so the plates clump together (flocculate) and sink faster; sinking flocs sweep up smaller particles on the way down.{{cite:sutherland2015}} The estuary’s circulation adds to the effect: in the northern Chesapeake, local resuspension of bottom sediment and an estuarine “sediment trap” created by the net circulation produce a turbidity maximum, a zone of especially muddy water.{{cite:schubel1968}} Nutrients are mixed and resuspended in the same zone, and many organisms use it as a nursery.{{cite:cbp_physical}}

Organic matter accumulation

Salt marshes are marshy because their ground is peat: decomposing plant matter, often several feet thick, that is waterlogged, root-filled and spongy. Smooth cordgrass is one of the main components of that peat.{{cite:noaa_est06}} As water filters through the marsh, grasses and peat trap excess sediments and nutrients along with pollutants such as pesticides and heavy metals.{{cite:noaa_est03}}

Oxygen in waterlogged sediment

Because marsh soils are waterlogged and full of decomposing plant material, oxygen levels in the peat are extremely low, a condition called hypoxia. Low oxygen favors bacteria that produce the rotten-egg smell of marshes and mudflats.{{cite:noaa_est06}} That smell is hydrogen sulfide, a plant toxin that builds up in oxygen-free marsh soils and interferes with how roots make energy and take up nitrogen.{{cite:koch1990}} Any marsh plant or burrowing animal has to solve this oxygen problem (see cordgrass and fiddler crab).

Why nutrient-rich sediment supports so much life

Rivers bring nutrients from the entire watershed into the estuary, where they fuel lush plant growth.{{cite:noaa_est03}} The organic-rich mud is food for deposit feeders: fiddler crabs, for example, scoop up mud and separate edible detritus from it using water pumped through their gills.{{cite:adw_uca}} Filter feeders such as oysters and ribbed mussels strain plankton from water passing over the bottom, and their wastes enrich the sediment further.{{cite:cbp_oyster,bertness1984}}
