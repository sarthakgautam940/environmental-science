Characteristic climate and conditions

An estuary’s “climate” is its water: how salty, how warm, how much river water flows in and how strongly the tides stir it. All four change from the river end to the sea, and from season to season.

Salinity: the master variable

Salinity is the amount of dissolved salt in water, usually given in parts per thousand (ppt), grams of salt per 1,000 grams of water.{{cite:cbp_physical}} Fresh river water is nearly 0 ppt, the ocean averages about 35 ppt, and estuary water ranges between 0.5 and 35 ppt.{{cite:noaa_est01}} Scientists divide this range into zones using the Venice System, adopted at a 1958 international symposium on brackish waters.{{cite:venice1958,freshwaterinflow}}

Freshwater input and river discharge

About half of the Chesapeake Bay’s water comes from the Atlantic Ocean and half is fresh water draining from its 64,000-square-mile watershed. Three rivers (the Susquehanna, Potomac and James) provide more than 80% of that fresh water, and the Susquehanna alone contributes about half (roughly 19 million gallons per minute).{{cite:cbp_facts}} Because river flow changes through the year, so does the Bay’s salinity: it is fresher in spring, when snow melts and heavy rainstorms are frequent, and saltier in drier months.{{cite:cbp_physical}}

How rainfall, evaporation and tidal mixing change conditions

  • River discharge and rainfall. A heavy spring rain or a big snowmelt pushes fresh water down-estuary and lowers salinity everywhere; in dry months, salt water moves farther up the estuary.{{cite:noaa_est05,cbp_physical}}
  • Density and layering. Fresh water is less dense than salt water, so it tends to float on top. The Chesapeake has a two-layer flow, warmer, fresher water moving seaward over saltier, denser water moving up-bay, separated by a zone of rapid salinity change called the pycnocline. Salinity therefore increases with depth.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
  • Tidal mixing. Most coasts have two high and two low tides each day, and every high tide pushes seawater into the estuary.{{cite:noaa_est01,noaa_est05}} How thoroughly fresh and salt water mix depends on wind, tidal range, the estuary’s shape, and the volume and speed of river flow.{{cite:noaa_est05}} In the Hudson River, tidal currents carry salt water more than 200 km upstream.{{cite:noaa_est05}}
  • Wind. Strong winds can mix the layers or even reverse the flow; northwest winds push water out and cause exceptionally low tides, while northeast winds pile water in.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
  • Evaporation. Where little fresh water flows in and evaporation is strong, estuaries can become hypersaline, saltier than the ocean, as in some lagoons along the Texas coast.{{cite:noaa_est06}}
  • Earth’s rotation. The Coriolis effect deflects flowing water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, so saltier water moving up the Chesapeake veers toward the eastern shore, making that side saltier.{{cite:cbp_physical}}

Temperature and the seasons

The Chesapeake Bay is shallow, its average depth, including tidal rivers, is only about 21 feet, so it cannot store much heat.{{cite:cbp_facts}} Its water ranges from 34 °F (1 °C) in winter to 84 °F (29 °C) in summer, and these changes control where underwater grasses grow and when fish and crabs feed, reproduce and migrate.{{cite:cbp_physical}}
