Intro

Where the river meets the sea

An estuary is a partly enclosed body of water where fresh water from rivers mixes with salty seawater. Everything that lives there must cope with conditions that change by the hour.

Estuaries and the wetlands around them are usually found where rivers meet the sea. Their water is brackish, a mixture of fresh water draining from the land and salty ocean water, and its saltiness can change from day to day with the tides and the weather.{{cite:noaa_est01}} Bays, sounds, lagoons and tidal river mouths can all be estuaries.

Estuaries are transitional zones that straddle land and sea, and fresh and salt water.{{cite:noaa_est01}} Water flowing in from the surrounding watershed brings nutrients that fuel lush plant growth, making estuaries “some of the most fertile ecosystems on Earth”, but the same water carries pollution, so they can also be among the most polluted.{{cite:noaa_est03}} Their marshes, mudflats, oyster reefs and seagrass beds filter water, buffer coasts against storm surge, and serve as nurseries for many fish and shellfish.{{cite:noaa_est03,cbp_physical}}

The big idea

Estuary life is shaped by four stresses: (1) salinity that rises and falls with every tide and season, (2) tides that alternately flood and expose habitats, (3) soft, waterlogged sediment that contains little or no oxygen, and (4) murky water that blocks light. The flip side is a constant supply of nutrients, which makes estuaries extraordinarily productive.

Quick reference for the class summary sheet, details and sources below

Type
Aquatic life zone (brackish water); includes salt marshes, mudflats, oyster reefs, seagrass beds and, in the tropics, mangroves
Location & latitude
Coasts of every inhabited continent, from the equator (Amazon) to over 50° N (Thames, Elbe); salt marshes at mid–high latitudes, mangroves in the tropics. Map ↓
Temperature
Follows the local climate but swings widely in shallow water: Chesapeake Bay ranges from 34 °F (1 °C) in winter to 84 °F (29 °C) in summer. Conditions ↓
Salinity & water
0.5–35 ppt; fresh at the river end, nearly seawater at the mouth; fresher in spring floods, saltier in dry seasons. Tides mix the water once or twice a day.
Substrate
Mud (silt and clay), sand and peat; rich in nutrients and organic matter but low in oxygen below a thin surface layer, with sulfide (“rotten-egg”) chemistry. Substrate ↓
Dominant producers
Smooth cordgrass and other salt-marsh plants (salt hay, glasswort), eelgrass, phytoplankton
Key animals
Eastern oyster, blue crab, fiddler crab, ribbed mussel, marsh periwinkle, mummichog, striped bass, herons, egrets, osprey
Key adaptations
Salt glands and salt-filtering roots; air channels to roots; osmoregulating gills; shell closing; burrowing; tide-timed behavior; filter feeding. Species ↓
Human problems
Fertilizer runoff and excess N & P → algal blooms and dead zones; sewage and industrial pollution; plastics; coastal development; dredging; sea-level rise; overfishing
Solutions
Farm runoff controls, sewage upgrades, the Chesapeake Bay “pollution diet,” marsh and oyster restoration, living shorelines, sustainable fishing, protected reserves and monitoring
